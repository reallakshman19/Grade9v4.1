1. Prerequisites and model boundaries

Use a steady ideal DC source and approximately ohmic positive resistors with temperature held fixed. Wires and junctions are ideal: a connected node has one potential. A crossing without a connection dot is not a junction. The voltmeter is ideal (draws negligible current), the ammeter is ideal (has negligible voltage drop), and external losses are ignored.

Before computing anything, identify which wire endpoints belong to the same two nodes. A parallel branch shares the voltage between those nodes, which is not necessarily the entire battery voltage.

2. Derive a parallel equivalent from current conservation

Let two positive ohmic branches R₁ and R₂ share node pair A/B. Because each branch sees the same node voltage V_AB, Ohm's law gives I₁=V_AB/R₁ and I₂=V_AB/R₂. At the node, conservation of charge gives I_total=I₁+I₂. Defining I_total=V_AB/R_p therefore yields 1/R_p=1/R₁+1/R₂. A third resistor upstream is in series with this equivalent block, so its voltage drop must be included separately.

3. Foundation worked anchor — source directly across two branches

An ideal 12 V supply is connected directly across 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors in parallel. Find each branch current, total current and the equivalent resistance.

  1. Both branch endpoints coincide at A/B. The ideal source directly establishes V_AB=12 V.
  2. Ohm's law: I₆=12/6=2 A; I₃=12/3=4 A.
  3. At A, I_total=2+4=6 A. So R_p=12/6=2 Ω.
  4. Check: 1/2=1/6+1/3. The parallel equivalent is smaller than the smallest positive branch resistance, 3 Ω.

4. Connected worked anchor — a series element changes the branch voltage

An ideal 10 V source drives a 2 Ω resistor in series with two 4 Ω branches in parallel. Find the upstream current, the block voltage and both branch currents.

  1. Only the two 4 Ω branches share A/B; reduce them to R_p=(4×4)/(4+4)=2 Ω.
  2. Series resistance is R_total=2 Ω+2 Ω=4 Ω; the source current is 10/4=2.5 A.
  3. The upstream 2 Ω resistor drops (2.5)(2)=5 V, leaving V_AB=10−5=5 V.
  4. Each 4 Ω branch takes 5/4=1.25 A. Their currents sum to 2.5 A, matching the measured series current.

Check the law at two places: 5 V+5 V=10 V by the loop voltage balance, and 1.25 A+1.25 A=2.5 A at the split junction. The diagram makes the two checks different.

5. Worked inverse construction — recover a missing branch

Different worked data from the fresh challenge: an ideal 20 V supply sends a measured 3 A through an upstream 4 Ω resistor into a downstream parallel pair. One branch has 8 Ω; the other is a finite positive resistor X. Find X and check the two conservation laws.

  1. Before the junction, the series element carries the measured 3 A, so its drop is (3 A)(4 Ω)=12 V.
  2. The two downstream branches therefore share V_AB=20−12=8 V. The known 8 Ω branch takes 8/8=1 A.
  3. Junction conservation gives 3−1=2 A through the unknown branch; X=8 V/2 A=4 Ω.
  4. Independent check: 8 Ω || 4 Ω = 8/3 Ω, and 4 + 8/3 = 20/3 Ω, hence source current=20/(20/3)=3 A.

Why this is inverse reasoning: current was observed before the junction; the unknown branch was inferred from the series voltage loss and the remaining junction current. Do not assume the known branch resistance must equal the unknown one.

6. Feasibility proof: which source-current measurements are physically possible?

For an ideal source V across a fixed positive series resistor R_s and a parallel block containing a fixed positive branch R_k and a finite, positive unknown X, its equivalent R_p=(R_k X)/(R_k+X) obeys 0 < R_p < R_k. Thus the source current must obey V/(R_s+R_k) < I_total < V/R_s. The direction of each inequality follows from dividing fixed positive V by a positive resistance.

In the 20 V, 4 Ω, 8 Ω worked setup, the physical interval is 5/3 A < I_total < 5 A. A reported 1 A or 6 A cannot be explained by adjusting only the unknown positive downstream resistor. If the measured current is exactly at an endpoint, at least one stated model condition has changed.

Boundary models: as X tends to infinity (open branch, not finite), the circuit approaches I_min=V/(R_s+R_k). As X tends to zero (short branch, not positive finite), R_p tends to 0 and I approaches I_max=V/R_s. Neither endpoint is attained by finite positive X. A real cell, wire, short or measurement error may violate the ideal model; that is not a license to assign a negative resistance.

7. Diagnose a plausible but invalid shortcut

Wrong model: “Any parallel branch receives all of the battery voltage.” This is true for the direct connection in figure A, but false for figure B. Ask which two endpoints define V_AB; then compute the voltage drop on every component before node A. If a learner argues that two equal-looking drawn paths are parallel, ask them to label and trace the wire nodes rather than trusting picture proximity.

Repair: Label node A and node B, reduce only the parallel pair, include the upstream resistor, then check both charge and energy conservation. This correction teaches the method without giving the later challenge's unknown-branch answer.

8. Inverse-and-feasibility exit check

An ideal 15 V source drives a 3 Ω series element into a parallel pair containing a known 6 Ω branch and an unknown positive branch. The source current is 3 A. Find the unknown resistance; is a measured 6 A possible under the same assumptions?

Show worked reasoning after attempting the exit check

Upstream drop=3×3=9 V, so V_AB=6 V. Known branch takes 1 A, unknown branch 2 A, and X=6/2=3 Ω. Limits: 15/(3+6)=5/3 A < I < 15/3=5 A for finite positive X; therefore 6 A is impossible. Check R_p=6||3=2 Ω and R_total=5 Ω, giving 15/5=3 A.

9. Unseen transfer

The accompanying Core1B does not ask for a new forward calculation with only swapped numbers. It reverses the direction of inference: the source current is measured, and the learner must infer a hidden branch.
