# PH12 — Magnetic Effects and Induction
## Continuous Core1A textbook manuscript · R2 author candidate

**Status:** AUTHOR_EXPOSED_DRAFT · SCIENCE_REVIEW_HOLD · NOT A PUBLISHED TEXTBOOK.  
**Curriculum:** Class X magnetic effects and domestic electricity, with motor/generator/induction concepts identified as **FORMATIVE** in the current CBSE treatment. Quantitative Faraday-rate laws and electromechanical terminal-velocity models are separately flagged **EXTENSION**.

### 1. A magnet's effect is observed through forces and alignment

Bring two bar magnets near one another. Their relative orientation affects whether corresponding poles attract or repel. A small compass needle tends to align with the local magnetic field. The compass shows **direction at its location**, not a physical string connecting two poles.

Magnetic fields can exist around permanent magnets and electric currents. A school diagram uses arrows and field lines to show how a test compass would orient; field lines are a representation of the field, not trajectories that particles must follow.

**Boundary:** Saying “field lines begin at north and end at south” describes the familiar external diagram for a bar magnet. The full magnetic field forms closed patterns, continuing through the material; isolated magnetic poles have not been observed in ordinary magnet experiments.

### 2. Reading a magnetic field diagram

The tangent to a drawn magnetic field line indicates local field direction. A denser field-line drawing is a qualitative convention for representing stronger fields, not a count of physical filaments. Field-line diagrams should not show intersecting lines assigning two distinct directions to the same point in a single well-defined field.

A compass near the north pole of a bar magnet generally points away from that pole externally, and near the south pole it points toward it. A student must establish where the compass is located before claiming left or right.

**Graphical diagnostic:** Two field lines cross at one point in a sketch, each with different arrows. Ask what direction a freely moving test compass should point at the crossing. The contradictory directions expose an invalid representation.

### 3. A current-carrying straight wire produces a field

A steady current in a long straight conductor creates a magnetic field that circles around the wire. The right-hand thumb rule relates conventional current direction (thumb) to the circulation sense (curled fingers). The rule must be applied from a declared viewpoint, especially for current coming out of or going into the page.

Dot and cross symbols are useful: a dot often means a vector toward the observer, like an arrow tip; a cross represents a vector away, like an arrow tail. Reversing the current reverses the field direction at every location while preserving the concentric geometry under the same idealization.

**Worked reasoning:** A straight wire passes perpendicularly through the page with conventional current toward the reader. The circulation is anticlockwise when viewed from the reader's side. At a point directly right of the wire, the field points upward on the page. Turning the current away reverses that direction.

### 4. Loops of current create an oriented field

For a circular loop, current elements produce a field that, near the center, is directed along the loop's axis. Use the right-hand grip convention to associate a direction around the loop with the direction of field along its normal. The loop must be oriented with its face and viewing side specified; “clockwise” alone is ambiguous if the observer moves to the opposite side.

Two loops can produce fields that reinforce or oppose one another near particular regions depending on their currents and geometry. A simple qualitative sketch is appropriate at Class X level; quantitative superposition off-axis is an additional theory problem.

### 5. A solenoid behaves like an electromagnet

A solenoid is a helical coil. When current passes through it, its field has a recognizable directional structure, and a long idealized solenoid has an approximately uniform interior field away from its ends. Its apparent north and south ends can be determined from winding direction and conventional current.

A change of wiring polarity can reverse the end-pole assignments. Adding a suitable ferromagnetic core changes field strength and response, but core behavior is not captured by “more current always means a proportionally stronger field” over every possible operating range.

**Transfer:** A coil is rewired with the battery polarity reversed while the same wound coil remains fixed. Predict what happens to the apparent poles, and distinguish the polarity reversal from simply rotating the page used to display the sketch.

### 6. Magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor

A wire carrying current in a magnetic field may experience a force. Qualitatively, the direction is perpendicular to the current direction and the relevant magnetic field direction. If the conductor is parallel to the field in the simple idealized setting, the magnetic force on the straight segment vanishes; orientation matters.

Fleming's left-hand rule helps keep field, conventional current and mechanical force directions in order. It is a mnemonic for the directional relationship, not a substitute for marking the three axes and considering a reversal.

**Worked reasoning:** Let conventional current point right, field point upward in the page, and adopt the usual \(\mathbf F\propto\mathbf I\times\mathbf B\) orientation. The force on the wire points toward the viewer, out of the page. Reverse current only and force reverses; reverse both current and field and the force points in its original direction.

### 7. Motor-force reversal can be tracked without guessing

An electric motor uses forces on current-carrying conductors within a magnetic field to create a turning tendency. A rectangular loop can experience forces on opposite sides that contribute a torque. To obtain sustained rotation in a simple DC motor, the current direction through the coil is reversed appropriately by a commutator as the coil rotates.

Students often memorize a picture with fixed red and blue poles and infer that the force on one side stays always upward. Instead, inspect which coil side has which current direction at each half-turn, and keep the external field direction fixed unless the problem explicitly changes it.

**Stage model:** Before the half-turn, mark current direction on both active sides. At the half-turn, determine the new contact arrangement and current through the sides. After the half-turn, check whether the turning tendency remains in the intended rotational sense.

### 8. Motors and generators are different energy conversions

A motor primarily converts electrical energy into mechanical motion; a generator uses mechanical driving to produce an electrical output. Both involve conductors and magnetic fields, but the energy input and controlled motion differ. A diagram showing a coil between magnetic poles is not enough to identify the device without its source or load and the direction of energy transfer.

A direct-current motor uses commutator action in a common school model. A basic alternating-current generator can produce a periodically changing polarity as its coil rotates. Terms like slip rings and split rings need to be associated with the actual circuit contact function rather than interchanged as memorized names.

### 9. Magnetic induction requires **change**

A strong magnet next to a stationary coil does not by itself establish continuous induced current. The essential trigger is a changing magnetic linkage with the circuit. Pushing a magnet toward a coil can create a transient induced electromotive effect; holding it fixed can remove that transient; pulling it away can reverse the effect.

A changing flux can occur without moving a magnet at all: a coil can rotate, the magnetic field source can vary, or the loop shape can change. Conversely, motion that leaves the linked flux unchanged may produce no simple induction outcome in the idealized model.

**Diagnostic:** A student says “a coil always has current whenever it is near a magnetic field.” Ask whether the circuit is closed and whether its linked field is changing. Those are separate necessary conditions in the standard qualitative picture.

### 10. Induced EMF is not the same as a measured current

A changing magnetic flux may induce an EMF around a loop. A closed conductive circuit can support current when a suitable path exists; an open circuit may have an induced potential but no steady current flowing through its broken path. This distinction becomes important when a voltmeter is present but the wire is disconnected.

For a simple magnet-and-loop demonstration, state the direction from which the coil is viewed before claiming clockwise or anticlockwise current. Without viewing convention and magnetic pole orientation, a bare directional word is ambiguous.

### 11. Lenz's law opposes the change, not necessarily the field

The qualitative Lenz direction rule says an induced response acts in a way that opposes the **change in linked magnetic flux** that produces it. It is incorrect to conclude that the induced magnetic field must always point opposite the original external field. If external linked flux is decreasing, the induced field tends to support the original direction to resist the decrease.

Consider a north pole approaching a loop. The linked field through the loop grows in magnitude for the appropriate orientation; the induced current creates an opposing field contribution. During retreat, the external linked field decreases; the induced response reverses relative to the approach case. The viewpoint must be held fixed throughout both descriptions.

**Conservation explanation:** A process that amplified the flux change without external work could create free energy in an ideal setting. Lenz's rule expresses the opposing feedback consistent with energy conservation. This is a conceptual warrant, not a full quantitative Faraday-law derivation.

### 12. Alternating current changes direction with time

Alternating current periodically reverses its direction. In a sinusoidal example with frequency \(f\), the current completes \(f\) cycles each second; the period is \(T=1/f\). A graph of voltage against time must show positive and negative values about a reference axis if it depicts signed alternating voltage. Direct current, by contrast, has a fixed sign in the simple school model.

Frequency by itself does not specify the peak value or actual power delivered to a circuit. A graph with twice as many cycles in the same window has higher frequency, not necessarily higher amplitude. In a generator, output depends on the coil motion and magnetic conditions as well as load; do not infer the exact waveform from a photograph alone.

**Worked anchor:** A 50 Hz periodic current completes one cycle in 0.020 s. A 25 Hz waveform has period 0.040 s. This computation addresses timing only; it does not determine the voltage amplitude.

### 13. Domestic wiring is primarily PH11-owned

Domestic electrical safety depends on how live, neutral and earth are connected, how loads are arranged, and how protection operates under faults. PH11 owns the canonical circuit-safety teaching record. PH12 should cross-reference it for the magnetic action of current or the supply's alternating nature, not duplicate the entire science record.

**Integrative task:** A fault current can operate an appropriate protective device. That does not imply that a magnet near an ordinary household wire will automatically induce a useful power source. Separate circuits, changing flux, and protection mechanisms must be distinguished.

### 14. Quantitative Faraday laws and motional EMF are enrichment

A detailed rate equation for magnetic flux change requires definitions of magnetic flux, area/orientation and applicable circuit geometry. Terminal velocity of a moving conducting bar under induced magnetic drag also requires mechanical-force and energy accounting. These are mathematically worthwhile **EXTENSION** problems but are not promoted to compulsory Class X merely because a candidate Core2 ledger mentions them.

A learner who knows the qualitative induction principle can already solve a demanding school-scope D3 task by deciding whether and how linked flux changes and by interpreting a sequence diagram carefully. A D4 candidate involving multiple changing conditions must be explicitly justified by a five-factor task review.

### 15. Experiment and evidence quality

An induction demonstration should record magnet position, direction of motion, coil continuity, meter polarity and viewpoint. A meter needle that moves briefly and returns to zero is evidence of transient response; interpreting its sign depends on how the instrument terminals are connected. Repeat the approach and retreat cases and check whether the sign reverses.

A photograph showing a magnet inside a coil but no motion record does not prove that current was induced during that moment. Distinguish an observation from a causal inference. This is the same reasoning discipline used in PH11's circuit measurements.

### 16. Chapter synthesis: field, current, motion, change

The chapter begins with representations of static magnetic fields, then fields produced by currents, then forces on current-carrying conductors, then electrical responses to changing magnetic linkage. A motor and a generator use these principles in opposite energy-conversion directions, but they are not interchangeable diagrams.

The chapter's first QRT targets are REPRESENT (translate 3D direction into a coherent diagram), EXPLAIN (trace why motion/change may cause a transient signal), and MODEL (choose between static-field, force-on-conductor and induction accounts). A separate SYNTHESIZE target can connect electrical and mechanical energy, but nontrivial D4 electromechanical calculations stay **EXTENSION/HOLD** until reviewed.

**Exit investigations:** Draw a magnetic field around a straight current carrying wire with the observer viewpoint explicit; explain a motor coil's continuing turning tendency; distinguish an open loop's induced EMF from current; and show why induced response can support an external field direction when linked flux is decreasing.
