PH07-01

Temperature, thermometers and calibration

Begin with measurement: a number on a thermometer must be connected to a calibrated physical state before any heat-transfer model is meaningful.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Phenomenon

Two materials at the same temperature may feel different; a repeatable thermometer may still be biased.

Scientific construction

A metal spoon and a wooden spoon left on the same table may feel different even when they are at the same temperature. Your hand responds to the rate at which energy is exchanged with it; the metal commonly conducts energy faster. Temperature describes thermal state, not the total internal energy or the rate of heat flow. Two bodies at the same temperature are in thermal equilibrium with each other when brought into thermal contact: no net heat transfer occurs. A contact thermometer therefore needs time to reach equilibrium with the sample, and should not appreciably alter that sample.

Thermometers use a reproducible temperature-dependent property, such as liquid-column length or electrical resistance. A digital reading is still the result of sensor calibration. A scale defines numerical labels, and a range defines where the instrument may be used. If 10 degrees occupy five equal intervals, each interval represents 2 degrees; count gaps, not marks. The Celsius and kelvin scales have equal-sized intervals but different origins: T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15. A rise of 8 °C is a rise of 8 K; the offset must not be applied to a temperature difference. Kelvin is written K, without a degree symbol.

For a linear sensor, let r be the raw reading and θ the temperature in °C. At reference baths (θ1,r1) and (θ2,r2), equal changes in θ produce equal changes in r. Thus (r−r1)/(r2−r1) = (θ−θ1)/(θ2−θ1), giving θ = θ1 + (r−r1)(θ2−θ1)/(r2−r1). The offset and the sensitivity (slope) are separate. If the instrument reads 5 at 0 °C and 85 at 100 °C, an indicated 45 corresponds to 50 °C. A third reference tests the linear assumption rather than proving it from the two endpoints. A finer display can still have a large systematic calibration error.

When measuring a liquid, choose an instrument whose range includes the expected temperature, immerse the sensing portion as specified, keep it clear of the vessel walls, wait for stability, and read the analogue scale at eye level. Do not remove a conventional laboratory liquid-column thermometer before reading: it begins exchanging energy with its new surroundings. Repeat readings to estimate scatter; check against a reference to investigate bias. A half-division rounding interval is only an ideal reading-resolution estimate, not a complete uncertainty budget. A clinical thermometer is intended for its specified body-temperature range, not a boiling-water experiment. Use safe digital or non-mercury laboratory instruments under supervision.

Build the model

  1. Establish a stable reference reading at each of two known temperatures. Why: Thermal equilibrium and known reference values anchor the sensor response.
  2. Construct normalized fractions of the reference interval. Why: For an affine response, equal fractions of raw and true-temperature intervals correspond.
  3. Check an unused reference and inspect resolution separately from bias. Why: Two endpoints always define a line; an unused measurement can contradict the assumption.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A scale has six marks from 10 to 30 °C inclusive. What is the interval? What is the fourth mark?

Five gaps span 20 °C, so each is 4 °C; the fourth mark is 22 °C.

Independent check: Count from 10: 10,14,18,22,26,30.

CONNECTED

A linear sensor reads 15 at 10 °C and 45 at 70 °C. It reads 25 in a bath. Infer the temperature.

Sensitivity is 30 raw units per 60 °C; 10 raw units correspond to 20 °C. Temperature is 30 °C.

Independent check: Substitute 30 °C into r=15+(30−10)/2=25.

ADVANCED

A sensor's raw readings at 0, 40 and 80 °C are 0, 23 and 40. Can an exact linear calibration fit all three?

The endpoints imply r=0.5θ, so 40 °C should give 20. The observed 23 contradicts exact linearity; without uncertainty or another response model, intermediate estimates are not certified.

Independent check: Compare slopes: 23/40 differs from 17/40.

Misconception and repair

A more precise display is automatically more accurate; subtracting a zero offset always fixes a scale.

Two instruments agree to a tenth on repeated readings but disagree with a certified reference. Which observation tests accuracy rather than repeatability?

Compare with independent references; distinguish offset, slope, resolution and scatter. Use an affine model only within its justified range.

Limits

  • Contact thermometer requires thermal equilibrium with the measured body, adequate immersion and negligible disturbance; do not touch container walls.
  • Two-point interpolation assumes linear response throughout the calibrated interval; two endpoints do not establish linearity.
  • Scale division and display resolution do not by themselves establish accuracy; extrapolation outside range is unsupported.
  • Reference ice/water points require appropriate purity, pressure and equilibrium; use known reference baths rather than improvising hazardous temperatures.

Exit check

A linear sensor reads 3 at 0 °C and 53 at 100 °C; its sample reads 18. What temperature follows? What if a 50 °C reference reads 29 exactly?

Check reasoning

30 °C conditionally; the third reading refutes exact linearity. θ=100(18−3)/50=30; at 50 °C the model predicts 28, not 29.

PH07-02

Heat, temperature, thermal equilibrium and steady energy balance

With a reliable temperature scale established, distinguish state from energy transfer. Equal temperatures, driven steady states and phase changes require different energy accounts.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Related existing Core2 foundation candidate — source/QRT HOLD. NSEJS-FND-CIRC-04 (electrical fuse model). This is a cross-topic thermal bridge; not an authenticated Heat exam or independently qualified D3/D4 question. The optional electrical scaling is a model, not practical fuse design. Exact candidate identity and coverage.

Phenomenon

A heater can maintain a body at constant temperature while it continuously loses heat.

Scientific construction

A cup of hot water left in a cooler room loses energy to its surroundings. The cup's temperature falls, but temperature and energy are not interchangeable quantities. Temperature describes thermal state and determines the direction of spontaneous net heat transfer. Internal energy is energy associated with the microscopic state of a system, and depends on how much matter is present and its state as well as temperature. A large amount of lukewarm water may contain more internal energy relative to a common reference than a small amount of hotter water. Heat is the energy transferred across a boundary because of a temperature difference, not a substance stored in a body.

When two initially unequal-temperature objects are placed in thermal contact, energy is spontaneously transferred from the hotter object to the cooler one. In an isolated pair with no external work, energy lost by one equals energy gained by the other. Their temperatures approach a common value. When they are in mutual thermal equilibrium, there is no net heat transfer between them. Equal temperature does not mean equal mass, equal total internal energy or no microscopic motion. A thermometer works because its sensor approaches equilibrium with the object. A temperature gradient within a conducting material is instead evidence of a possible heat-flow path.

A system may maintain a constant temperature for another reason. A heated plate in a room may receive energy electrically while continuously losing energy to the air and surrounding surfaces. For the chosen plate as system, conservation of energy gives ΔU=E_in−E_out, neglecting other energy stores and mechanical work; the rate form is P_in−P_out=dU/dt. If input exceeds loss, internal energy rises. At a steady temperature with unchanged phase and material state, internal energy is constant, so input and output rates balance. This is a driven thermal steady state, not thermal equilibrium with the cooler room. If the heater stops, the previously balanced state does not persist. The direction of spontaneous heat loss is still from hotter plate to cooler surroundings.

Many advanced problems involve comparing rates at a specified target temperature rather than finding a final equilibrium temperature. Suppose uniform panels of the same material are kept at the same fixed surface temperature in identical surroundings. If heat loss per unit exposed area is the same, the total loss is proportional to exposed area. Doubling exposed area doubles the necessary input power under those assumptions. A cylindrical surface has lateral area 2πrL, while its cross-section is πr². These are different geometric measures. A process depending on cross-section must not be confused with one depending on exposed surface. In a cross-topic electrical model, resistance of a uniform wire is proportional to L/r² and electrical input power is I²R. At fixed temperature, a surface loss proportional to rL can be balanced against input, yielding I²/r² proportional to r and hence I proportional to r^(3/2). This is an optional conceptual bridge to PH11-10: it assumes fixed resistivity, uniform wire temperature, neglected end losses, and identical thermal boundary conditions. It is not a safe engineering design rule and does not authorize fuse experiments.

A final distinction: at a phase transition a body can gain internal energy while its temperature stays constant, even without balancing an equal heat loss. Therefore constant temperature alone does not prove energy-rate balance. Establish the system, the energy paths, and whether phase or other state variables change before choosing a model. PH07-06 and PH07-11 develop these limitations.

Build the model

  1. Choose the physical system and draw energy paths across its boundary. Why: Conservation relates changes of internal energy to transfers and work across that boundary.
  2. Compare input and output powers; integrate over a constant-rate interval. Why: Power is energy per unit time; net power controls internal-energy accumulation.
  3. Distinguish zero net transfer between contacting bodies from balanced nonzero rates. Why: A steady temperature with constant internal energy can coexist with a continuous energy flow.
  4. Compare surface and cross-sectional geometric measures only under specified conditions. Why: Distinct mechanisms depend on distinct geometric variables; model assumptions justify scaling.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A warm ceramic tile transfers 90 J to a cooler metal tile in an otherwise isolated pair. What are the energy changes?

Warm tile ΔU=−90 J; cooler tile ΔU=+90 J, assuming no other stores or work.

Independent check: Their summed internal-energy change is zero.

CONNECTED

An unchanged-phase plate receives 42 W and loses 30 W. Over 15 s the rates stay constant. Find its energy change and temperature trend.

Net rate is 12 W, so ΔU=180 J. Temperature rises if the heat capacity is positive and no other state variable changes.

Independent check: Input 630 J minus loss 450 J equals 180 J.

ADVANCED

Two identical-temperature flat plates have exposed areas 0.20 m² and 0.50 m². The first needs 24 W to remain steady. If area-specific loss and all other conditions are equal, what input maintains the second?

Loss flux=120 W/m². Second plate needs 60 W; it is in a driven steady state, not equilibrium with the room.

Independent check: 60/0.50=24/0.20=120 W/m².

Misconception and repair

Constant temperature means no heat transfer; all equal-temperature objects have equal internal energy.

A heater continuously supplies power to an unchanged-temperature plate. Where does that energy go? Does equal temperature tell you the amount of matter?

Separate thermal state from internal energy and transfer; balance energy rates and check phase.

Limits

  • Heat is energy transferred because of temperature difference; internally generated energy or electrical work is not itself heat transfer.
  • Thermal equilibrium between bodies in contact means no net heat flow between them; a driven steady state can sustain nonzero energy fluxes.
  • Use P_in−P_out=dU/dt for the stated system; P in watts (J/s), U in joules.
  • Inferring temperature change from internal-energy change assumes unchanged phase and positive heat capacity; detailed calorimetry belongs to PH07-11.
  • For power-law surface loss, area proportionality assumes fixed surface temperature, ambient conditions, surface properties and heat-loss flux per unit area. This is a model, not a general fuse law.

Exit check

A steady warm solid receives 18 W and loses 18 W. It is hotter than the room. Is it in equilibrium with the room? What happens to its internal energy over 20 s?

Check reasoning

No; internal-energy change is zero under the unchanged-state assumptions. Input and loss are each 360 J; nonzero heat flow persists across a temperature difference.

PH07-03

Conduction: contact paths, material stacks and thermal leaks

Now follow one transfer path through matter. Geometry, contact and the temperature difference determine whether the conductive model applies.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A small metal fastener can bypass an otherwise effective layer of insulation.

Scientific construction

Heat conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions in matter. Neighbouring particles exchange energy; in metals mobile electrons can contribute strongly. A solid does not need to flow as a whole to conduct. A conductor transfers energy relatively readily, while an insulator transfers it less readily under comparable geometry and conditions. Neither is a source of cold or heat. At equal temperatures, there is no net conductive heat transfer between bodies in equilibrium.

Touching metal and wood at the same room temperature can feel different because the rate of energy transfer from a warmer hand differs. The sensation also depends on contact, geometry and the material's thermal response, not just its temperature. A thermometer tests the temperature claim; controlled heat-flow measurements test the transfer-rate claim.

For a simple steady one-dimensional slab, with constant thermal conductivity k, thickness L, area A and temperatures fixed on its faces, the rate of heat flow is P=kAΔT/L. This quantitative relation is supplied here as enrichment, not a claim that it is compulsory NSEJS content. Define thermal resistance R=L/(kA); the temperature drop across the slab is PR. This resembles a flow-resistance model but concerns energy per time, not material current. The temperature drop is not heat stored in the slab. Doubling thickness doubles resistance under fixed k and A; doubling area halves it.

If layers lie one after another along the same path and there is no storage or side loss at their junction, the steady power through each is equal. Their temperature drops add, so R_total=R₁+R₂. A contact interface can add resistance even when its thickness is negligible; do not assume perfect thermal contact unless stated. If two independent paths connect the same hot and cold surfaces, each experiences the same endpoint temperature difference. Their powers add: P_total=ΔT/R₁+ΔT/R₂. An insulating wall with a metal bolt can therefore lose substantial energy through a small bypass. A low-resistance parallel route does not simply add to the series resistance of the insulating layers.

For a heat-loss investigation, compare the same boundary temperatures, area, contact pressure and steady-state condition. If a sample is still warming, the input power need not equal the transmitted conductive power. Radiation and convection can provide other paths. A model fitted to two conditions is not validated merely because it reproduces those conditions; an unused change of geometry or temperature can test the model. Inverse tasks may have several possible combinations of material/contact/bypass resistances. Identify the additional observation that would distinguish them before claiming a material property. No matching conduction question has been established in the inspectable Core2 bank; the constructions here are authored transfer.

Build the model

  1. Draw hot and cold boundaries and every physical connection. Why: Conduction is driven by a temperature difference, and geometry determines which paths exist.
  2. For a series branch, add temperature drops at common power. Why: Steady conservation with no side losses gives the same power through sequential layers.
  3. For parallel branches, calculate separate powers at the common boundary temperature difference. Why: Energy transfer rates add at the hot boundary; a bypass can dominate.
  4. Test the model with an independent change or internal temperature observation. Why: One total resistance alone does not identify all individual resistances.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Two equal-area slabs of the same material differ only in thickness: 2 cm and 4 cm. Compare steady power at the same face temperatures.

The thicker slab has twice the resistance and half the power.

Independent check: P∝1/L under the stated model.

CONNECTED

Two layers R=2 and 3 K/W are in series across 20 K. Find power and temperature drops.

R_total=5 K/W, P=4 W; drops 8 K and 12 K.

Independent check: Drops sum to 20 K.

ADVANCED

A 5 K/W insulation path and a 10 K/W bolt path share 30 K. Find total loss and what happens if the bolt is removed.

P_ins=6 W, P_bolt=3 W, total 9 W; removing bolt leaves 6 W if conditions unchanged.

Independent check: Effective R=30/9=3.333 K/W, less than either branch.

Misconception and repair

All resistances always add, and metal feeling colder proves lower temperature.

Does a bolt lie along the insulation path or connect the same boundaries independently? What would a thermometer show?

Draw actual paths before combining resistances; check boundary temperatures independently.

Limits

  • Steady linear one-dimensional conduction; k constant over the stated range, no storage or unmodelled side losses.
  • Series layers share power only at steady state without side paths; parallel paths share endpoint temperatures.
  • Contact resistance and radiation/convection require separate accounting where relevant.
  • Surface sensation is not a direct measurement of temperature or conductivity.

Exit check

Two series resistances 4 and 2 K/W connect 30 °C to 12 °C. An independent 9 K/W bypass spans the same boundaries. Find total power.

Check reasoning

5 W. Series branch 18/6=3 W; bypass 18/9=2 W.

PH07-04

Convection in fluids: buoyancy, circulation and model selection

Conduction does not require bulk motion. In fluids, density and buoyancy can create circulation and another energy-transfer route.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Supporting prerequisite · existing Core2 candidate · source/QRT HOLD. NSEJS-FND-CIRC-04 (NSEJS-FND-CIRC-04). This candidate is producer-labelled D2, not admitted D3/D4 evidence. Cross-topic thermal foundation, no verified official exam locator. Pinned source identity · Primary related Core1A.

Phenomenon

A fluid heated from below can circulate, while comparable heating from above may produce stable layering.

Scientific construction

Conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions without a bulk flow of matter. Convection transfers energy by the motion of fluid parcels. A fluid parcel that is warmer than neighbouring fluid often expands at approximately the local pressure, making its density smaller. In a gravitational field the surrounding fluid exerts a buoyant force equal to the weight of displaced fluid. When that force exceeds the parcel's weight, an upward tendency results. A cooler denser parcel may tend downward. This is a model of natural convection; it depends on the equation of state, gravity and surrounding density, not on a universal slogan that every hot object rises.

Imagine two layers of the same ordinary fluid. If the lower layer is warmed and becomes less dense than the upper layer, a small upward displacement can allow it to continue rising; denser fluid replaces it below. A return flow is needed because matter is conserved in a closed container. If the lighter layer already lies above the denser layer, small vertical displacements instead tend to be opposed by buoyancy. This stable arrangement can reduce natural convection even when the layers have different temperatures. Heat can still transfer by conduction or radiation.

A fan or pump can drive flow even without the density contrast that sustains natural convection. This is forced convection. When moving air passes a warm surface, energy can be transferred between the surface and air, then carried away. Faster flow often increases heat loss under comparable conditions, but an observation of faster cooling alone cannot identify the exact mechanism: evaporation, area, radiation or initial temperatures might differ. The existing Core2 fuse candidate assumes lateral heat loss and neglects end conduction. Treat that as a conditional model; this unit teaches the convection prerequisite and a separate experimental reasoning construction.

For a small parcel of volume V surrounded by fluid of density ρ_s, the simplified net vertical force relative to weight is (ρ_s−ρ_p)gV upward, before drag or other constraints. Do not turn this into a terminal-speed prediction without resistance and geometry. In water close to 4 °C the ordinary monotonic density-temperature relation can fail, and mixtures can have salinity or composition gradients. Establish which layer is actually denser. A credible explanation identifies the energy path, the density contrast, the buoyancy direction, the return path and the limits of the model.

Build the model

  1. Compare actual density of a parcel with its surroundings at the same level. Why: Buoyant force follows displaced fluid weight, not temperature alone.
  2. Determine direction of net buoyancy and stability of layer arrangement. Why: A displaced parcel rises if lighter than its surroundings and sinks if heavier.
  3. Close the flow path and trace energy transported with matter. Why: Mass is conserved in a closed vessel; rising flow requires compensating return flow.
  4. Distinguish natural and forced convection and test alternative heat-loss paths. Why: A pump adds mechanical forcing; conduction and radiation can persist without circulation.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A parcel of volume 0.002 m³ has density 980 kg/m³; its surroundings have density 1000 kg/m³. Use g=10 m/s². Find net buoyancy.

(1000−980)×10×0.002=0.4 N upward before drag.

Independent check: Parcel weight 19.6 N; buoyant force 20 N.

CONNECTED

A vessel contains a lighter upper layer and denser lower layer. An identical vessel has the lighter layer below. Which is more susceptible to natural mixing?

The second: lighter fluid tends to rise and heavier fluid descends. First is stable against small vertical displacements.

Independent check: Swap a parcel mentally across the interface and check its restoring or destabilizing buoyancy.

ADVANCED

Two equal dry warm plates cool in still air and fan-driven air. Initial temperatures, areas and surroundings match. Fan-driven plate needs 30 W more input to remain at the same temperature. What follows?

Net extra loss is 30 W under steady balance. Forced convection is a plausible cause, but separate radiation and any changing conductive path before assigning the whole difference.

Independent check: An independent fan-off repeat or heat-flux measurement can test reproducibility.

Misconception and repair

Warm fluid always rises regardless of surrounding density; every faster cooling observation proves convection.

A warm parcel is denser than its surroundings because of dissolved material. Which way does buoyancy act?

Use measured densities and separate mechanisms through controls.

Limits

  • Ordinary fluid expands with warming only over the specified regime; water near 4 °C and composition gradients require actual density data.
  • Parcel buoyancy assumes local hydrostatic surroundings and a small parcel; drag, boundaries and pressure fields affect flow.
  • Natural convection needs gravity, a destabilizing density arrangement and freedom to move; forced convection need not.
  • Heat loss may combine multiple paths; rate comparisons require controlled area, temperature and environment.

Exit check

A parcel is 1.5 kg/m³ less dense than neighbouring air and has volume 0.04 m³, g=10 m/s². What is its simplified net buoyancy?

Check reasoning

0.6 N upward. Density difference × volume × g; no prediction of terminal speed without drag.

PH07-05

Radiation, surface absorption and differential heating

Convection needs matter; radiation does not. Put absorbed power, energy storage and competing losses into a common balance.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Supporting prerequisite · existing Core2 candidate · source/QRT HOLD. NSEJS-FND-CIRC-04 (NSEJS-FND-CIRC-04). This candidate is producer-labelled D2, not admitted D3/D4 evidence. Cross-topic thermal foundation, no verified official exam locator. Pinned source identity · Primary related Core1A.

Phenomenon

Surfaces under equal illumination can warm at different rates and drive different heat exchanges.

Scientific construction

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic energy transfer. It does not require matter between source and receiver; sunlight crosses space. A surface absorbs some of the incident radiation, reflects some and, if not opaque, may transmit some. In an idealized opaque case the absorbed fraction α and reflected fraction sum to one. Visible colour alone does not fix absorption or emission at every wavelength. Surface properties, the spectrum of illumination and geometry matter.

A body's temperature changes when energy accumulates in it. At fixed phase and approximately constant effective heat capacity C, internal-energy change is CΔT. If incident power P_inc is steady, absorbed power is αP_inc, and other losses are approximately constant over a short interval, the initial warming rate is (αP_inc−P_loss)/C. This makes a crucial distinction: a faster warming surface might absorb more power, have smaller thermal capacity, lose less energy, or combine these factors. Equal incident radiation does not establish equal absorbed radiation; equal absorbed power does not establish equal warming rate. At a steady temperature, absorbed power balances outward losses (and other transfers if present).

Radiative exchange can coexist with convection and conduction. In a model comparison, change one mechanism at a time, measure input and output if possible, and check whether any assumed constant rate remains reasonable. Thermal emission and radiative cooling can depend strongly on surface temperature, so a fixed loss over a large range is generally unjustified. The thermal component of the existing Core2 fuse question uses a fixed-temperature surface-loss assumption. This unit is a supporting thermal bridge, not a direct authenticated exam link.

Land and sea illustrate coupled surface heating and convection. Under simplified daytime conditions land may warm faster than water because effective thermal capacities, mixing, evaporation and energy exchange differ. Air near the warmer surface may warm, become less dense and rise; a pressure distribution and return flow can then sustain a near-surface breeze from cooler sea toward warmer land. At night land may cool more quickly, reversing the thermal contrast and potentially the local circulation. Real winds also depend on weather, pressure gradients and geography. A difference in surface temperature alone does not determine wind speed or guarantee the direction in an arbitrary situation.

To reason from measurements, separate the radiation absorbed from the net energy retained. Two warm-up observations at different incident powers can distinguish a fixed-loss model from a pure absorption model if C is independently known, or infer α and loss under stated assumptions. A further observation tests the fit. Without enough independent measurements, multiple models remain compatible; explicitly identify the missing variable rather than assigning causation to colour.

Build the model

  1. Identify incident, reflected, absorbed and outgoing energy paths. Why: Conservation distinguishes incident power from retained energy.
  2. Write a short-interval temperature-rate model with a stated thermal capacity. Why: Net power accumulates as internal energy under unchanged phase and constant C.
  3. Use independent observations to separate absorption and losses rather than identify them from one rate. Why: A single temperature slope supplies one constraint on multiple unknowns.
  4. Connect surface heating to local density-driven motion only under the stated atmospheric conditions. Why: Convection and pressure gradients require more information than surface temperature alone.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

An opaque plate absorbs 60% of 100 W incident radiation. What is absorbed and reflected power?

60 W absorbed, 40 W reflected.

Independent check: Sum equals incident 100 W.

CONNECTED

Two plates each absorb 40 W and lose 10 W initially. Capacities are 300 and 600 J/K. Find rises in 20 s.

Net energy 600 J, rises 2 K and 1 K. Equal absorption does not give equal warming.

Independent check: CΔT=600 J for both.

ADVANCED

A plate with C=200 J/K warms initially at 0.10 K/s with 80 W incident and 0.20 K/s with 120 W incident. Assume fixed loss and α. Infer α and loss.

Net powers 20 and 40 W. Difference 20=40α, α=0.5. Loss=40−20=20 W. Predict 0.15 K/s at 100 W.

Independent check: At 120 W, 60−20=40 W agrees.

Misconception and repair

The darker object must heat faster solely because it absorbs more radiation.

A plate absorbs more power but also has a larger thermal capacity. What quantity actually predicts initial temperature rise?

Compare net power divided by capacity and test assumptions independently.

Limits

  • Opaque surface assumption only where stated; absorption/emission depend on spectrum and surface.
  • Constant C, fixed phase and approximately constant loss are short-interval model assumptions.
  • Steady power balance excludes net energy accumulation only if system state is unchanged.
  • Land/sea-breeze model is qualitative and conditional on background weather and local density/pressure response.

Exit check

A surface absorbs 36 W and initially loses 12 W. C=240 J/K. Find its initial warming rate.

Check reasoning

0.10 K/s. Net power 24 W divided by 240 J/K.

PH07-06

Phase changes: particle models and constant-temperature transitions

The previous balances assumed unchanged state when relating net energy to temperature. Phase coexistence provides a critical counterexample.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Related existing Core2 prerequisite · HOLD. NSEJS-FND-CIRC-04 assumes a fixed melting-temperature heat-loss balance; it is not a phase-transition examination admission. Pinned source identity.

Phenomenon

A pure substance can absorb energy while its temperature stays nearly constant during a change of state.

Scientific construction

Solids, liquids and gases contain moving particles. In a solid, particles generally oscillate about relatively fixed positions; in a liquid they can rearrange and flow; in a gas they are widely separated on average and move freely between collisions. Heating usually increases the temperature within a single phase. It can also change the arrangement and interaction energy of particles. A phase transition is not a chemical change merely because the physical properties change.

For a pure substance at a specified pressure, solid and liquid can coexist at a melting temperature. With a slow supply of energy, an equilibrium mixture may remain at that temperature while more solid becomes liquid. On freezing, energy is released to surroundings while the liquid fraction decreases. At a specified pressure, a pure liquid can boil when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding pressure; vapour bubbles can persist within the bulk liquid. Condensation reverses the liquid-to-vapour transition. Evaporation occurs at the surface even below the boiling point and will be distinguished in PH07-07. Melting and boiling temperatures are not universal for all materials, and a mixture may change phase over a range.

Draw a temperature-versus-time graph for a pure sample initially below its melting point with constant positive net heating. Temperature rises to the melting temperature, remains approximately level during coexistence, rises again once the solid is gone, and can reach the boiling plateau if the energy supply continues and pressure is fixed. During a plateau, net energy input need not be zero. It may increase the enthalpy of the system through changed phase fraction; at fixed pressure expansion work is also relevant. Do not assume every horizontal segment means phase change. A heater and heat loss may balance at a steady temperature, the heater may be off, or the instrument may be unresponsive. A phase observation or independent energy measurement distinguishes these possibilities.

When cooling, the sign of net energy transfer reverses. Ideal equilibrium freezing may also produce a plateau while latent energy leaves the sample. In actual observations, supercooling, mixtures, pressure changes and thermal gradients can alter curves. Heating rate changes the duration of a plateau when net energy input changes; it does not necessarily change the equilibrium transition temperature. For example, with constant net power P, the energy delivered over an interval Δt is PΔt. If identical samples undergo the same transition, twice the net power gives half the transition time in this idealization. Quantitative latent-heat treatment is enrichment for PH07-12, not presumed compulsory here.

PH07-07

Evaporation, cooling and competing environmental mechanisms

Vaporization at a surface adds another energy path. Distinguish it from boiling and from ordinary convective cooling.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A damp surface can become cooler than a nearby dry surface even when both receive the same airflow.

Scientific construction

Liquid molecules have a distribution of energies. At a free surface, some molecules escape to the gas phase even when the liquid is below its boiling temperature. The energy needed for vaporization comes from the liquid and surroundings; remaining molecules can redistribute energy and the liquid can cool. Boiling is different: vapour bubbles form in the bulk when the relevant vapour-pressure condition is met. Surface evaporation does not require boiling and does not occur only at one temperature.

Evaporation and condensation occur together at a liquid-vapour interface. In an open environment, vapour can be carried away, sustaining a net outward mass flux. In a closed space, vapour may accumulate until evaporation and condensation balance dynamically. Relative humidity describes how much water vapour is present relative to saturation at the air temperature; it is not simply the total amount of water in the air. Warmer liquid, larger exposed area and airflow may increase net evaporation under otherwise matched conditions. Airflow can also change convection, and the effect of humidity depends on liquid and air temperatures.

Consider two equally warm plates, one dry and one wet, under identical airflow. Both may transfer heat to surrounding air. The wet plate additionally can lose energy through evaporation. If the plates are kept at the same temperature by heaters, the extra required input may estimate the additional evaporation-related loss only if all other paths are matched or separately measured. The strongest evidence combines mass-loss data with energy and temperature observations; a temperature difference alone cannot identify a unique mechanism.

A useful controlled comparison is a dry reference and an otherwise equivalent wet sample at each airflow setting. The dry reference indicates non-evaporative heat loss at matched temperature and geometry; the wet-dry difference is a candidate evaporative term. If the rate of liquid mass loss is known and no droplets escape, an independently supplied energy per unit mass of vaporization permits a check. Real wet surfaces may also differ in emissivity or convective properties, so subtracting a dry reference is a model with conditions, not automatic proof.

For example, suppose a dry reference requires 12 W at steady temperature and a wet reference requires 22 W. If the wet surface evaporates 0.004 g/s and the supplied vaporization energy is 2500 J/g, evaporation accounts for 10 W, consistent with the difference. If a second airflow setting produces a larger wet-dry power difference without a matching increase in measured evaporation, then additional mechanisms or measurement error must be investigated. Build the model

  1. Identify the liquid surface, vapour environment and mass-transfer paths. Why: Net evaporation is surface escape minus condensation.
  2. Trace the energy needed for phase change and other heat-transfer paths. Why: Cooling results from net energy imbalance, not simply mass loss.
  3. Construct a controlled dry/wet or airflow/humidity comparison. Why: Changing one variable and observing mass as well as temperature helps separate competing mechanisms.
  4. Check the measured energy difference against independently measured mass loss. Why: Agreement supports the conditional model; discrepancy identifies a missing path or uncertainty.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Two identical wet cloths are exposed to dry air and humid air at matched temperature and airflow. Which tends to dry faster?

The dry-air case, if the liquid-vapour driving condition is larger and other factors match.

Independent check: Measure mass lost in equal intervals rather than judge by touch.

CONNECTED

A dry plate needs 8 W and an otherwise matched wet plate 14 W to stay at the same temperature. Interpret the difference.

6 W extra loss is consistent with evaporation if other heat paths match; not proof without mass data.

Independent check: Check wet mass loss and a supplied vaporization-energy coefficient.

ADVANCED

Evaporation estimate is 7.2 W; 1.8 W unexplained by that model. Check wet/dry path mismatch, droplet loss and uncertainty.

Independent check: Compare 7.2 W prediction against independent 9 W measurement.

Misconception and repair

Evaporation requires boiling; increased wind cooling proves evaporation is the only cause.

A fan cools a dry object too. How can dry/wet comparisons plus mass loss distinguish evaporation from convection?

Match temperatures and paths, use dry controls and compare energy accounting with actual evaporation.

Limits

  • Open versus closed system and net evaporation versus gross molecular exchange must be distinguished.
  • Mass loss measures evaporation only when other losses such as spills/droplet entrainment are negligible.
  • Dry-wet subtraction requires matched non-evaporative heat paths; wetting may change them.
  • Use supplied vaporization coefficient only as an approximate local model, not a constant for all liquids/temperatures.

PH07-08

Heating and cooling curves: reconstructing energy and state histories

Combine these ideas in time-dependent observations: a temperature graph measures state, while a power graph describes energy flow.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

Two samples can have identical temperature-time curves while receiving different energy inputs.

Scientific construction

A temperature-time graph shows a state measurement, not a direct measurement of energy. A rising trace indicates increasing temperature, a falling trace indicates decreasing temperature, and a horizontal segment indicates constant measured temperature. To infer energy flow we need to know the sample state, its thermal capacity and the energy paths. In a fixed-mass single phase at constant effective capacity C, a net energy change CΔT corresponds to a temperature change. Over a short interval with constant net power, the slope is P_net/C. This model is an authored quantitative extension of qualitative heating and cooling curves.

The same slope can be produced by larger power and larger capacity or by smaller power and smaller capacity. Two equal slopes are not proof of equal absorbed power. If input power is known but heat loss is not, the graph determines net power only when C is known. A steady plateau with an active heater might have equal input and loss; a phase-change plateau can have positive net input. A sensor response lag can also distort an apparent transition. Phase identity and independent energy measurements matter.

For piecewise constant rates, divide the graph at changes in heater setting, loss, state or slope. Use the slope in each unchanged-phase segment to recover net energy rate if capacity is known. For a time interval, the energy transferred is the area under a power-time graph, not the area under a temperature-time graph. When power varies, add the energy in each interval, or use an appropriate integral. A negative net power gives a falling temperature under the stated single-phase model; it is not negative absolute temperature.

An inverse experiment can use multiple controlled runs at the same initial temperature. If the sample remains single phase, capacity C and a common constant loss L give slopes s₁=(P₁−L)/C and s₂=(P₂−L)/C. Subtracting eliminates L, giving C=(P₂−P₁)/(s₂−s₁), provided the slopes differ and assumptions hold. Then L=P₁−Cs₁. An unused run tests the fitted model. If its slope disagrees, the initial model is not supported as one common C and L; do not simply discard the third result.

During a cooling curve, losses often decrease as the sample approaches ambient temperature. A curved trace can thus result from changing loss without changing phase or capacity. Conversely, a phase transition can change heat capacity or produce a plateau. A graph is evidence but does not by itself identify the mechanism. This unit follows PH07-06, which teaches phase coexistence; here the decisive reasoning is reconstruction of independent temperature and power histories. No authenticated matching Core2 item has been located in the inspectable bank.

Build the model

  1. Partition the trace at slope, state or power changes. Why: A single formula need not describe every segment.
  2. Connect temperature change to net energy only in the specified single phase. Why: CΔT is a model with state assumptions, not the area under T(t).
  3. Build a separate power-time account for the intervals. Why: Integrating power over time gives energy; sign distinguishes accumulation and loss.
  4. Fit and test an inverse model with independent observations. Why: Two equations may identify two parameters under assumptions; a third tests them.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A solid with C=200 J/K warms 3 K in 30 s at constant net power. Find energy and power.

600 J and 20 W.

Independent check: Slope 0.1 K/s ×200 J/K=20 W.

CONNECTED

A heater supplies 30 W for 40 s and then 10 W for 20 s. Constant loss is 10 W; sample stays single phase with C=200 J/K. Describe T.

First net 20 W, rise 4 K; second net zero, plateau 20 s with no phase change.

Independent check: Input 1400 J, loss 600 J, net 800 J.

ADVANCED

Initial slopes at heater powers 40 and 70 W are 0.10 and 0.25 K/s. Fit common C and loss.

C=30/0.15=200 J/K; L=40−20=20 W. At 50 W slope predicts 0.15 K/s.

Independent check: Check both fitted powers and reserve an unused run.

Misconception and repair

A flat temperature trace proves a phase change; a steeper trace proves more gross heater power.

Could an unchanged-phase sample with equal heater and loss powers have a plateau? Could a smaller sample warm more steeply?

Separate temperature, phase and power data before interpreting slopes and plateaus.

Limits

  • Fixed mass, composition and phase and approximately constant effective C for the slope equation.
  • Input and loss rates measured independently where possible; no assumption of constant power over an entire graph.
  • Phase plateaus require phase-state and pressure evidence; sensor lag and gradients can distort traces.
  • Common-loss inverse fit applies only to matched initial conditions and independently changed inputs.

Exit check

An unchanged-phase sample at C=400 J/K warms at 0.05 K/s with heater 35 W. Estimate loss.

Check reasoning

15 W. Net=20 W, loss=35−20=15 W.

PH07-09

Thermal expansion: gaps, constraints and relative motion

Temperature can also change dimensions. Track free expansion and constraints separately from the energy model.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A bridge needs clearance to expand without forcing its supports to move.

Scientific construction

Heating generally changes the average separation of particles in a solid. Many solids expand when warmed and contract when cooled, although some materials and temperature ranges behave differently. Temperature is a state variable; expansion is a change of geometry. A hole in a freely heated uniform plate normally enlarges as though the missing material had expanded with the rest of the plate. It is not correct to imagine material expanding only into the hole while its outer boundary is fixed unless that is a stated mechanical constraint.

For small temperature changes in a uniform freely expanding rod, the approximate change in length is ΔL=αL₀ΔT. This is an optional quantitative model. The coefficient α depends on material and temperature, and a rod with twice the initial length expands twice as much for the same α and ΔT under the model. A negative ΔT gives a negative change in length when α is positive. Convert metres to millimetres before comparing an expansion with a small clearance.

A change in length does not specify the position of either end. A rod anchored at its left end moves its right end by ΔL. If anchored at its right end, the left end moves in the opposite direction. A symmetrically supported rod whose centre is fixed moves each end by half its total change. For two rods facing each other across a gap, draw both anchors and free ends. If both ends move into the gap, add their advances when calculating the reduction in clearance. If both ends move in the same direction, the change in gap depends on their relative displacement. A signed coordinate diagram is more reliable than a universal instruction to subtract or add expansions.

The free-expansion equation predicts an unconstrained change. Once a gap closes against a rigid stop, that free movement is no longer available. Continuing to calculate a negative physical gap would be a model error; additional heating can produce stress, deformation or buckling depending on the system. Quantitative stress requires stiffness, constraint and material data that are not supplied by α alone. Similarly, a bimetallic strip bends because bonded layers would undergo different free expansions; the detailed curvature depends on geometry and stiffness, not merely the larger expansion coefficient. A freely heated uniform plate and a constrained layered strip require different mechanical models.

To solve a clearance problem, sketch the cold geometry, define positive direction, compute unconstrained changes, locate the moving edges, and find the temperature at which contact first becomes possible. Check the model separately before and after that boundary. This subtopic is a thermal foundation with authored advanced transfer; no matching exact Core2 item has been established in the inspected bank.

Build the model

  1. Identify initial geometry, free edges and anchors. Why: Length change alone does not specify end displacement.
  2. Compute signed free change using the supplied coefficient and temperature interval. Why: Positive expansion coefficient and positive ΔT imply increased free length.
  3. Translate each edge motion into a common gap coordinate. Why: Clearance is the difference between edge positions.
  4. Find the contact threshold and state the mechanical model limit. Why: Once clearance reaches zero, constraints change the physical situation.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A freely expanding 2 m rod has α=10×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ and warms 50 K. Find its change in length.

0.001 m = 1 mm.

Independent check: The fractional change is 0.0005.

CONNECTED

Two rods facing each other expand 0.8 and 1.2 mm into a 3 mm gap. What clearance remains?

1 mm.

Independent check: The gap reduction is the sum 2 mm because both advances point into it.

ADVANCED

A centre-fixed rod expands 2 mm in total next to a rigid stop 0.7 mm from its right end. Can the unconstrained prediction be realized?

No: right end would advance 1 mm, exceeding clearance. Contact happens at 0.7 mm end advance; later stress needs another model.

Independent check: Free total expansion at first contact is 1.4 mm.

Misconception and repair

Every rod end moves by its whole length change, and negative calculated clearance is a real physical gap.

Where is the anchor? What prevents two solids from occupying the same place after contact?

Draw signed endpoint motions, calculate first contact, and distinguish free expansion from constrained response.

Limits

  • Uniform temperature and material, small strain, approximately constant α for free expansion.
  • Fixed anchors and specified supports; actual displacement depends on constraint geometry.
  • After contact, the free-expansion model no longer predicts physical interpenetration or stress.
  • Bimetal bending direction assumes bonded layers and a suitable unconstrained strip; curvature needs mechanical data.

Exit check

Two end-fixed rods advance 0.4 mm and 0.7 mm into a 1.5 mm gap. Find remaining clearance.

Check reasoning

0.4 mm. Subtract the two advances from the initial gap.

PH07-10

Thermal experiments: fair comparisons, insulation and uncertainty

Use the preceding mechanisms to design fair comparisons and interpret measured differences without overclaiming causes.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

Two containers can cool at different rates without the difference being caused by their insulation.

Scientific construction

A fair thermal experiment starts with a question that can be answered by a measured quantity. To compare insulation, for example, decide whether the quantity is energy loss at a fixed temperature difference, or the time taken by a matched sample to cool through a stated interval. The two are related but not identical. Equal initial temperatures do not imply equal initial internal energy or equal thermal capacity. Equal containers may have different lids, contact paths or exposed areas. Control variables must be chosen because of their physical relevance, not merely because a laboratory checklist mentions them.

Consider two warm water containers. Their cooling rates may depend on water mass, vessel heat capacity, exposed surface, wall thickness, airflow, evaporation, radiation and ambient temperature. If the sample masses differ, comparing temperature slopes does not directly compare energy-loss rates. In the single-phase approximation, effective heat capacity C includes the participating water and vessel; loss power with no input is approximately −CΔT/Δt over a short interval. When heater power is adjusted to maintain a constant temperature, the net accumulation is approximately zero and the input can estimate total loss, but only if other energy paths and instrument power are accounted for.

A stronger experiment uses matching specimens at the same initial temperature, geometry and ambient conditions, changes just the proposed treatment, and repeats the comparison with positions exchanged to test a location effect. Use temperature probes with known calibration and response time; record the times and readings rather than rounding each step. If evaporation is plausible, measure mass loss or use a suitable matched cover. If wall conduction is the target, a metal support may create a separate thermal bypass. A result from a whole container does not isolate a material's thermal conductivity.

Graphs show measurements and the model used to interpret them. Plot temperature against time with units, indicate uncertainty or resolution, and compare slopes over the same temperature range rather than assume cooling is linear over an entire experiment. Independent power data permit a check on a temperature-based estimate. When two uncertainty intervals overlap, a simple interval comparison cannot establish a strict ordering; repeated observations may reduce random error but do not remove common calibration bias. If the prediction fails, look at the control conditions and the model before selecting a cause.

The advanced reasoning is to reconstruct what each measurement can establish, find a confounded comparison, and design a new observation that discriminates alternatives. For example, swapping two treatments between locations can separate a treatment effect from a location effect when the effects are additive and stable. A full factorial comparison at matched temperature can test the additive assumption itself. None of these authored tasks is claimed as an authenticated NSEJS question; the inspectable Core2 bank contains no exact match.

Build the model

  1. State the measured outcome and the physical hypothesis. Why: A cooling slope and a total heat-loss power answer different questions.
  2. List energy paths and relevant variables; choose a matched control. Why: A confounder can produce the same apparent treatment effect.
  3. Use independent measurements to test the proposed model. Why: Capacity, power, mass loss and location swaps constrain alternative explanations.
  4. Report observed differences, uncertainty and the limits of causal inference. Why: A fit or repeat alone does not establish a universal material property.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Equal-phase specimens cool at 0.10 and 0.15 K/s, with capacities 300 and 200 J/K. Compare inferred losses.

Both are 30 W under the stated model.

Independent check: Multiply capacity by slope magnitude; temperature rate alone was misleading.

CONNECTED

A 300 J/K container loses 2 K in 30 s with heater off. Estimate average net loss.

20 W.

Independent check: 300×2/30=20 W; use matched temperature interval.

ADVANCED

At matched T, treatment A and B require 12 and 18 W at location X. A requires 15 W at location Y. Under additive stable effects predict B at Y.

21 W. Treatment effect +6 W, location effect +3 W.

Independent check: Measure B at Y independently to test additivity.

Misconception and repair

A faster temperature fall proves poorer insulation, and repeated readings eliminate every error.

Could a smaller heat capacity yield faster cooling at the same loss power? Could all probes share one offset?

Use matched state, independent energy and control data, and distinguish repeatability from calibration.

Limits

  • Use a fixed-phase, approximately constant effective capacity only over the stated comparison interval.
  • Match initial state, temperature range, geometry, ambient conditions, probe calibration and relevant transfer paths.
  • An additive treatment/location model requires stability and no interaction; a fourth cell can test it.
  • Repeatability does not establish accuracy; interval comparisons are conditional on the stated uncertainty model.

Exit check

Two loss estimates are 18±2 W and 21±2 W. Do these bounds alone establish the second is strictly larger?

Check reasoning

No; intervals 16–20 and 19–23 W overlap. Admissible values can reverse the ordering; need improved precision or another test.

Specific heat and calorimetry: energy-balance inverse problems

Optional extension: convert qualitative thermal capacity into a quantitative energy account for unchanged-phase mixing.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

EXTENSION — optional higher-level material. Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

Equal masses of different materials can require different energy to warm by the same temperature interval.

Scientific construction

EXTENSION — quantitative calorimetry is higher-level enrichment, not established compulsory NSEJS material. Heat is transferred energy; temperature is a state variable. Specific heat capacity c describes how much energy per unit mass changes temperature by one kelvin under specified conditions and within one phase. The heat capacity C of a particular sample equals mc when c is approximately constant. Two samples of the same material can have different capacities because their masses differ. For an unchanged-phase sample, ΔU≈mcΔT when other work and energy stores are appropriately accounted for.

In an ideal insulated mixing experiment, the hotter body transfers energy to the cooler body until they reach a common equilibrium temperature. If no energy crosses the system boundary and no phase change occurs, the sum of their signed energy changes is zero. For two bodies with capacities C₁ and C₂, C₁(T_f−T₁)+C₂(T_f−T₂)=0, so T_f=(C₁T₁+C₂T₂)/(C₁+C₂). The result is capacity-weighted, not necessarily the arithmetic mean. It must lie between the initial temperatures when all capacities are positive and there is no external input or phase change. This check is not sufficient by itself to prove the model.

A calorimeter can absorb energy too. Include its own capacity and initial temperature rather than silently assign all transferred energy to the liquid. If the system loses E_loss to surroundings, the sum of signed changes is −E_loss. A temperature measurement alone may not separate unknown heat capacity from unknown energy loss. In a calibration experiment use a known energy input, or a known hot and cold body, and independently check leakage or repeat at changed conditions. Two independent experiments can solve two unknown parameters if their equations are genuinely independent; an extra experiment tests whether the proposed common parameters remain valid.

For a model with an unknown vessel capacity C_v and unknown constant energy loss E_loss, a known hot sample supplies energy H and a known liquid absorbs L. The difference H−L equals C_vΔT_v+E_loss. Plot that difference against the vessel's temperature rise: the slope is C_v, intercept E_loss, under the stipulated common-loss model. A negative inferred capacity is physically impossible for the ordinary positive-capacity model; a negative inferred loss could indicate net external input, a sign error or failed assumptions. Changes in duration and temperature can change loss; constant E_loss is a hypothetical comparison model, not a general law.

Use a system boundary and sign convention. Draw initial and final states, list every participating body, and check that energy lost equals energy gained plus accounted losses. If the result crosses a transition temperature, the no-phase model is invalid; PH07-12 develops the piecewise extension. The planning register also names an unbound PH04 prerequisite; this unit supplies its own elementary energy-accounting bridge without claiming PH04 qualification. Build the model

  1. Draw the system boundary and identify each body's initial and final state. Why: Energy conservation requires all participating components.
  2. Write a signed term C_i(T_f−T_i) for each unchanged-phase component. Why: Positive sign means internal energy increases under the model.
  3. Include external energy and solve the balance; test temperature bounds. Why: A weighted mean is valid only for a closed no-phase system.
  4. Use independent calibration observations to distinguish capacity and heat leak. Why: Independent equations and a physical check are necessary for inverse identification.

PH07-12 · OPTIONAL EXTENSION

Latent heat, composite calorimeters and thermal engines

Optional extension: extend the balance across phase boundaries and distinguish cyclic engine work from rejected heat.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

EXTENSION — optional higher-level material. Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

Ice can absorb a large amount of energy without warming above its melting temperature while some ice remains.

Scientific construction

EXTENSION — latent-heat calorimetry and thermal engines are higher-level comparisons, not established compulsory NSEJS content. An unchanged-phase sample changes temperature when it gains or loses energy, as modelled in PH07-11. During equilibrium melting at fixed pressure, adding energy can instead change the amount of liquid while temperature remains at the melting point. The latent heat of fusion L_f is energy per unit mass for melting under specified conditions. For vaporization the corresponding coefficient is L_v. These energy requirements are different from specific heat: Q=mL involves a phase fraction change, whereas Q=mcΔT describes a temperature interval within a phase.

A composite calorimeter with ice and warm water must be solved by considering possible final states before applying a single equation. If ice begins below its melting point, energy first warms the ice to that point. Then energy is required to melt it. Only after all ice has melted can the resulting liquid warm above the melting temperature under the usual ideal equilibrium conditions. Warm water, vessel and any other bodies supply or absorb energy according to their own temperature changes. Do not apply one liquid specific heat to an interval crossing solid, melting and liquid stages.

For a useful decision procedure, first calculate energy available from warm bodies cooling to the transition temperature, including vessel capacity and accounted external transfer. Compare it with energy needed to warm the cold solid to the transition temperature and melt it. If enough energy reaches the transition but not enough melts all solid, the final equilibrium has both phases at the transition temperature; calculate the melted mass from the remaining energy. If exactly enough melts all solid, the final liquid can be at the transition temperature. If extra energy remains, solve the final liquid warming balance including all participating capacities. If energy is insufficient even to reach the transition, another sub-transition state model may be needed; for water, cooling/freezing of initially liquid water can matter. Do not use the simplified procedure outside its stated regime.

A different kind of energy balance applies to a cyclic heat engine. During a complete cycle the working substance returns to its initial state, so its internal energy change is zero. For a model receiving heat Q_in and rejecting Q_out, net work delivered is W_out=Q_in−Q_out. Thermal efficiency is W_out/Q_in. An ordinary heat engine operating between thermal reservoirs cannot convert all heat from one reservoir into net work in a cycle; the second law constrains it, not just the first-law balance. This unit uses energy accounting and efficiency only; detailed Carnot bounds and cycles are not required. An engine's rejected heat can warm a separate body, but that is another system; count energy transfer once, not as both work and heat.

The advanced task is selecting the phase regime or energy-system boundary, then checking a proposed observation independently. A final temperature at melting does not reveal whether all ice has melted without phase or energy evidence. A claimed engine efficiency does not follow from output work alone unless input is known. These authored constructions are not direct exam admissions; no exact Core2 item was located.

Build the model

  1. List phases and initial temperatures; identify possible final regimes. Why: Sensible heating and latent energy are different terms.
  2. Compare energy available at transition temperature with energy required for phase conversion. Why: The comparison determines whether a mixed-phase or all-liquid model is valid.
  3. Solve only the appropriate regime and verify phase fraction and energy balance. Why: A negative melt mass or an above-transition equilibrium with remaining ice is inconsistent under assumptions.
  4. For an engine draw separate heat input, rejection and work arrows over a cycle. Why: The working substance returns to its initial state; rejected energy is not work.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

0.05 kg ice at0 °C melts completely; L_f=334000 J/kg. Find required energy.

16700 J.

Independent check: Mass × latent coefficient.

CONNECTED

Warm water plus vessel releases10020 J cooling to0 °C. Ice at0 °C has mass0.05 kg; L_f=334000. Find final state.

0.03 kg melts, 0.02 kg ice remains; equilibrium0 °C.

Independent check: Available energy <16700 J needed for all ice.

ADVANCED

An engine absorbs2000 J and rejects1500 J per cycle. Find work and efficiency.

500 J, 25%.

Independent check: Energy balance 2000=1500+500; not all heat becomes work.

Misconception and repair

Every calorimetry task can be solved using one mcΔT equation; an engine can turn all input heat into work because energy is conserved.

Can ice remain at 0 °C with added energy? Does conservation alone establish a physically possible cyclic engine?

Choose phase regime before calculating; distinguish first-law accounting from second-law feasibility.

Limits

  • Explicit EXTENSION; phase-change and engine models are not established compulsory content.
  • Pure ice/water at stated transition temperature and pressure, equilibrium, no evaporation unless specified, supplied constant coefficients.
  • Check energy regime before assuming all ice melts; simplified examples start ice at 0 °C and sufficient energy to reach coexistence.
  • Engine first-law accounting is over a complete cycle; second-law constraints and device efficiency require separate physical conditions.
