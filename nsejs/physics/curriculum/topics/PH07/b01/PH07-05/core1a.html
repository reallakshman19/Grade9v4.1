Phenomenon

Surfaces under equal illumination can warm at different rates and drive different heat exchanges.

Scientific construction

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic energy transfer. It does not require matter between source and receiver; sunlight crosses space. A surface absorbs some of the incident radiation, reflects some and, if not opaque, may transmit some. In an idealized opaque case the absorbed fraction α and reflected fraction sum to one. Visible colour alone does not fix absorption or emission at every wavelength. Surface properties, the spectrum of illumination and geometry matter.

A body's temperature changes when energy accumulates in it. At fixed phase and approximately constant effective heat capacity C, internal-energy change is CΔT. If incident power P_inc is steady, absorbed power is αP_inc, and other losses are approximately constant over a short interval, the initial warming rate is (αP_inc−P_loss)/C. This makes a crucial distinction: a faster warming surface might absorb more power, have smaller thermal capacity, lose less energy, or combine these factors. Equal incident radiation does not establish equal absorbed radiation; equal absorbed power does not establish equal warming rate. At a steady temperature, absorbed power balances outward losses (and other transfers if present).

Radiative exchange can coexist with convection and conduction. In a model comparison, change one mechanism at a time, measure input and output if possible, and check whether any assumed constant rate remains reasonable. Thermal emission and radiative cooling can depend strongly on surface temperature, so a fixed loss over a large range is generally unjustified. The thermal component of the existing Core2 fuse question uses a fixed-temperature surface-loss assumption. This unit is a supporting thermal bridge, not a direct authenticated exam link.

Land and sea illustrate coupled surface heating and convection. Under simplified daytime conditions land may warm faster than water because effective thermal capacities, mixing, evaporation and energy exchange differ. Air near the warmer surface may warm, become less dense and rise; a pressure distribution and return flow can then sustain a near-surface breeze from cooler sea toward warmer land. At night land may cool more quickly, reversing the thermal contrast and potentially the local circulation. Real winds also depend on weather, pressure gradients and geography. A difference in surface temperature alone does not determine wind speed or guarantee the direction in an arbitrary situation.

To reason from measurements, separate the radiation absorbed from the net energy retained. Two warm-up observations at different incident powers can distinguish a fixed-loss model from a pure absorption model if C is independently known, or infer α and loss under stated assumptions. A further observation tests the fit. Without enough independent measurements, multiple models remain compatible; explicitly identify the missing variable rather than assigning causation to colour.

Build the model

  1. Identify incident, reflected, absorbed and outgoing energy paths. Why: Conservation distinguishes incident power from retained energy.
  2. Write a short-interval temperature-rate model with a stated thermal capacity. Why: Net power accumulates as internal energy under unchanged phase and constant C.
  3. Use independent observations to separate absorption and losses rather than identify them from one rate. Why: A single temperature slope supplies one constraint on multiple unknowns.
  4. Connect surface heating to local density-driven motion only under the stated atmospheric conditions. Why: Convection and pressure gradients require more information than surface temperature alone.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

An opaque plate absorbs 60% of 100 W incident radiation. What is absorbed and reflected power?

60 W absorbed, 40 W reflected.

Independent check: Sum equals incident 100 W.

CONNECTED

Two plates each absorb 40 W and lose 10 W initially. Capacities are 300 and 600 J/K. Find rises in 20 s.

Net energy 600 J, rises 2 K and 1 K. Equal absorption does not give equal warming.

Independent check: CΔT=600 J for both.

ADVANCED

A plate with C=200 J/K warms initially at 0.10 K/s with 80 W incident and 0.20 K/s with 120 W incident. Assume fixed loss and α. Infer α and loss.

Net powers 20 and 40 W. Difference 20=40α, α=0.5. Loss=40−20=20 W. Predict 0.15 K/s at 100 W.

Independent check: At 120 W, 60−20=40 W agrees.

Misconception and repair

The darker object must heat faster solely because it absorbs more radiation.

A plate absorbs more power but also has a larger thermal capacity. What quantity actually predicts initial temperature rise?

Compare net power divided by capacity and test assumptions independently.

Limits

  • Opaque surface assumption only where stated; absorption/emission depend on spectrum and surface.
  • Constant C, fixed phase and approximately constant loss are short-interval model assumptions.
  • Steady power balance excludes net energy accumulation only if system state is unchanged.
  • Land/sea-breeze model is qualitative and conditional on background weather and local density/pressure response.

Exit check

A surface absorbs 36 W and initially loses 12 W. C=240 J/K. Find its initial warming rate.

Check reasoning

0.10 K/s. Net power 24 W divided by 240 J/K.
