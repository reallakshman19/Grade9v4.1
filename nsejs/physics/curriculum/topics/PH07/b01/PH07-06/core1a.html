Phase changes: particle models and constant-temperature transitions

Phenomenon

A pure substance can absorb energy while its temperature stays nearly constant during a change of state.

Scientific construction

Solids, liquids and gases contain moving particles. In a solid, particles generally oscillate about relatively fixed positions; in a liquid they can rearrange and flow; in a gas they are widely separated on average and move freely between collisions. Heating usually increases the temperature within a single phase. It can also change the arrangement and interaction energy of particles. A phase transition is not a chemical change merely because the physical properties change.

For a pure substance at a specified pressure, solid and liquid can coexist at a melting temperature. With a slow supply of energy, an equilibrium mixture may remain at that temperature while more solid becomes liquid. On freezing, energy is released to surroundings while the liquid fraction decreases. At a specified pressure, a pure liquid can boil when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding pressure; vapour bubbles can persist within the bulk liquid. Condensation reverses the liquid-to-vapour transition. Evaporation occurs at the surface even below the boiling point and will be distinguished in PH07-07. Melting and boiling temperatures are not universal for all materials, and a mixture may change phase over a range.

Draw a temperature-versus-time graph for a pure sample initially below its melting point with constant positive net heating. Temperature rises to the melting temperature, remains approximately level during coexistence, rises again once the solid is gone, and can reach the boiling plateau if the energy supply continues and pressure is fixed. During a plateau, net energy input need not be zero. It may increase the enthalpy of the system through changed phase fraction; at fixed pressure expansion work is also relevant. Do not assume every horizontal segment means phase change. A heater and heat loss may balance at a steady temperature, the heater may be off, or the instrument may be unresponsive. A phase observation or independent energy measurement distinguishes these possibilities.

When cooling, the sign of net energy transfer reverses. Ideal equilibrium freezing may also produce a plateau while latent energy leaves the sample. In actual observations, supercooling, mixtures, pressure changes and thermal gradients can alter curves. Heating rate changes the duration of a plateau when net energy input changes; it does not necessarily change the equilibrium transition temperature. For example, with constant net power P, the energy delivered over an interval Δt is PΔt. If identical samples undergo the same transition, twice the net power gives half the transition time in this idealization. Quantitative latent-heat treatment is enrichment for PH07-12, not presumed compulsory here.

The existing Core2 fuse candidate uses a fixed melting-temperature energy balance for a wire. It is a related phase-state prerequisite, not an authenticated phase-transition question. The distinction between a phase-changing sample and an unchanged-state steady-temperature wire is essential: maintaining a constant temperature alone does not establish that net energy transfer is zero.

Build the model

  1. Identify whether the substance is single phase or in phase coexistence. Why: The same temperature can correspond to different phase fractions.
  2. Trace energy direction and phase change together. Why: Energy absorbed or released can change state without raising or lowering equilibrium temperature.
  3. Compare net input over the interval and look for an independent phase observation. Why: A horizontal graph alone is not proof of latent energy transfer.
  4. Test pressure, purity and heating/cooling assumptions. Why: Transition temperatures and curve shapes depend on the physical conditions.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

An ice-water mixture at 0 °C receives energy slowly at ordinary pressure. What changes first?

The ice fraction decreases while the equilibrium temperature remains near 0 °C until ice is gone.

Independent check: Observe both phases rather than use a temperature reading alone.

CONNECTED

Two equal pure samples melt completely at net input powers 10 W and 20 W. First plateau lasts 120 s. Predict the second plateau.

Both transitions require the same energy under matched conditions; 10×120=20×t, so t=60 s.

Independent check: Both receive 1200 J of net energy during melting.

ADVANCED

A heated pure sample has a flat temperature segment and no visible phase change. It receives 15 W and loses 15 W. Is melting established?

No. Balanced rates in unchanged phase provide an alternative explanation. Check phase fraction or independent heat input/loss.

Independent check: The net input is zero under the stated rate balance.

Misconception and repair

Every temperature plateau means melting or boiling, and constant temperature proves no energy enters.

Compare a melting sample with a dry plate held at a fixed temperature by equal input and loss. Which extra observation discriminates the models?

Combine phase-state observations and energy-flow data; do not infer phase or net energy from temperature alone.

Limits

  • Pure substance, fixed pressure and near-equilibrium coexistence for an ideal transition plateau.
  • Distinguish net energy transfer, expansion work and phase change; temperature alone does not establish ΔU.
  • Constant net input for time-ratio comparisons; equal mass/material and identical initial/final phase fractions.
  • Mixtures, supercooling, pressure changes and sensor limitations can alter observed curves.

Exit check

An equilibrium liquid freezes at constant pressure while losing energy. Does its temperature necessarily fall throughout the transition?

Check reasoning

No; solid fraction can grow at the fixed freezing temperature. Energy leaves while the phase changes; subsequent solid cooling can lower temperature.
