Thermal emission versus reflected color

Scientific construction

A surface can reflect incident light and emit thermal radiation. Its ordinary visible color describes reflected light under illumination; it does not by itself specify spectral emissivity at high temperature. At fixed temperature and comparable geometry, thermal radiance in a given band is emissivity in that band times blackbody radiance. In a supplied graybody model with equal area and environment, larger emissivity means larger net radiative power when hotter than surroundings. A coating can change with temperature, and total infrared power need not rank visible brightness. Therefore the textbook black-surface rule requires a stated spectral or gray approximation. Work with simulated data, not incandescent objects.

Worked foundation

At one common temperature, two opaque model surfaces have visible-band emissivities0.8 and0.3. Which has greater emitted radiance in that band?

Worked construction

Two equal-temperature panels have identical visible-band emissivity but different infrared emissivity. Does equal visible brightness establish equal total radiated power?

No; spectral emissivity elsewhere can differ. Measure calibrated emission over relevant bands.

Limits and repair

Specified ideal or matched conditions only; supplied quantitative relations do not establish compulsory exam scope. Identify where you replaced spectral measurement by a color label or confused radiation with total energy loss. State the operating band, temperature, emissivity and comparison conditions; provide compatible alternative and a discriminator.
