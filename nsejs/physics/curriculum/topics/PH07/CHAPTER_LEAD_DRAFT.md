# PH07 — Surface heating, radiation and air circulation

**AUTHOR-EXPOSED CORE1A LEAD DRAFT · NOT COMPLETE CHAPTER · ACADEMIC HOLD**

## Begin with an observation

On a sunny afternoon the ground often becomes warmer than nearby water, and an onshore breeze may develop.

## Build the physical idea

Light delivers energy to land and sea. The temperatures attained depend not only on the incoming radiation but on absorption, heat capacity, mixing, evaporation and exchange with the air. In a simplified daytime scenario land warms faster, the adjacent air tends to warm and become less dense, and a pressure-driven circulation can bring cooler air inland near the surface. A textbook account must trace that whole mechanism; a slogan such as 'hot air rises' does not establish wind direction in every arrangement. At night the relative heating pattern may change. A photograph, temperature record or schematic needs a reference time, direction and weather conditions before any circulation prediction is defensible. This model is qualitative: without rates, geometry and meteorological data we cannot calculate a numerical wind speed.

## Derive the model, not a shortcut

1. **Describe the energy entering each exposed surface and the pathways by which it leaves.** Why valid: Energy absorbed need not equal temperature rise; energy can be stored, transported or lost.

2. **Connect a conditional temperature difference to changes in adjacent air density and pressure.** Why valid: For comparable pressure and gas composition, warmer air generally has lower density; the resulting pattern must be considered spatially.

3. **Trace the consequent possible surface circulation while noting pressure and wind conditions.** Why valid: Airflow requires pressure gradients and is influenced by surrounding weather, so the proposed direction is conditional.

## Three worked tiers

### Foundation

**Question:** Two identical trays, one dark and one light, receive similar illumination. Which measurements would test an absorption hypothesis?

**Reasoning:** Use comparable trays, exposure and thermometer placement; track time-temperature curves and acknowledge different losses.

**Independent check:** Repeat with positions exchanged to test location bias.

### Connected

**Question:** A coastal temperature log shows a warmer inland surface than sea water during daylight. Explain a possible near-ground air motion.

**Reasoning:** Under suitable pressure conditions, air flowing from sea toward land replaces rising warmer air inland.

**Independent check:** Check the predicted return flow aloft and compare with recorded wind direction.

### Advanced

**Question:** Two surfaces produce different final temperatures but receive different wind cooling. Can colour alone explain the result?

**Reasoning:** No. Wind is a confounder, so the causal claim is not established until exposure and cooling are controlled.

**Independent check:** Swap the samples and repeat under comparable airflow; do not infer absorptivity from one endpoint.

## Conditions and limitations

- Surface colour alone does not determine heat capacity or resulting wind direction
- Sea-breeze reasoning is a daytime idealization; pressure gradients and geography matter
- Heating and cooling do not imply a fixed rate without an energy-transfer model

## Diagnose a plausible mistake

**Wrong idea:** A dark surface always produces a sea breeze irrespective of wind or thermal storage.

**Diagnostic:** If warm land air rises, what establishes the horizontal surface flow direction?

**Repair:** Identify the pressure response and its assumptions; surface colour is only one input to the energy balance.

## Exit question

Why can equal illumination fail to produce equal temperature rises?

**Answer:** Surfaces can differ in absorption, heat capacity and cooling/mixing rates. A temperature change reflects net energy retained relative to heat capacity, not incident radiation alone.
