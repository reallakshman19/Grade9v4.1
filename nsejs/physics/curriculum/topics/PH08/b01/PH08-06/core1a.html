Pitch, loudness, amplitude and intensity

Phenomenon

Two sounds can have the same pitch but different measured amplitude and perceived loudness.

Scientific construction

A vibrating source produces an oscillation; a sensor may record displacement, pressure, or an electrical signal proportional to one of them. In a symmetric sinusoid, amplitude is the maximum deviation from the equilibrium value, half the peak-to-peak span. A graph's horizontal repeat interval determines period and frequency. A tall graph does not imply a higher frequency. Pitch describes perception of frequency (especially the fundamental of a simple tone), whereas loudness depends on intensity, frequency sensitivity, duration, listening conditions and the listener.

Sound intensity is the average power transported per unit area normal to propagation. For a linear progressive sinusoidal plane wave in the same medium, mean intensity is I=p_rms²/(ρv), where p_rms is the root-mean-square excess pressure, ρ is density and v is sound speed. For a sinusoid p_rms=p_peak/√2. Thus two matched pressure measurements in the same medium have intensity ratio (p₁/p₂)². Pressure and displacement amplitudes are not interchangeable. In the same medium a displacement amplitude A gives pressure amplitude proportional to fA for a linear travelling sinusoid; comparing different frequencies by displacement amplitude alone is insufficient. The latter relations are explicitly enrichment, not asserted compulsory.

A separate geometric model applies to an ideal isotropic source in an unbounded lossless medium: power crosses a spherical area 4πr², so intensity P/(4πr²). That is an inverse-square relation, not a rule that all spaces or sources obey. Under those conditions and a fixed medium, pressure amplitude is proportional to 1/r. In rooms, reflections, directivity, absorption or interference can defeat this model. If two different-frequency tones are averaged over sufficiently long intervals, their mean intensities may be added when cross terms average out; coherent same-frequency waves cannot always be treated that way.

The reasoning job is to decide what the sensor measured, separate frequency from amplitude, and select a propagation model supported by the conditions. A ratio of pressures is not a ratio of powers. A ratio of intensities does not establish the ratio of perceived loudness. The existing Core2 sound-wave candidate requires interpreting displacement amplitude; this unit prepares that prerequisite and extends it with original advanced intensity comparisons.

Build the model

  1. Read the graph's vertical physical quantity and equilibrium baseline. Why: Amplitude must describe a specified field; pressure, displacement and voltage are different quantities.
  2. Recover period from matching phase and separate it from amplitude. Why: Frequency uses temporal repetition, not vertical height.
  3. Select pressure-intensity or geometric spreading model under its explicit conditions. Why: Energy transported per area and wave impedance govern the pressure relation; conservation through spheres gives ideal spreading.
  4. Check a second observation or identify missing perceptual/medium information. Why: A numerical intensity does not uniquely establish perceived loudness.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A sinusoidal pressure trace has extrema +3 Pa and −3 Pa with a period of 5 ms. Find peak pressure and f.

Peak pressure 3 Pa, f=200 Hz.

Independent check: Peak-to-peak is 6 Pa; the full cycle repeats five milliseconds later.

CONNECTED

Two sinusoidal waves in the same medium have peak pressures 2 and 6 Pa. Compare their intensities.

I₂/I₁=(6/2)²=9.

Independent check: RMS pressure ratio is also 3, so square gives 9.

ADVANCED

An ideal isotropic source gives intensity 0.08 W/m² at 2 m. Find intensity at 4 m and pressure-amplitude ratio.

I(4)=0.02 W/m²; p(4)/p(2)=1/2 in the same medium.

Independent check: Intensity multiplied by spherical area is constant at both locations.

Misconception and repair

Taller wave graphs have higher pitch; twice the pressure amplitude means twice the intensity or twice the perceived loudness.

Compare two equal-period pressure traces with different amplitudes. Which measurements alone are enough to rank frequency, intensity and perception?

Read independent graph dimensions, square pressure ratio under matched impedance and keep perceptual claims conditional.

Limits

  • Sinusoidal signals and calibrated linear sensors for peak-to-peak inference; use the appropriate zero/reference level.
  • Pressure squared intensity relation assumes a progressive linear wave and common impedance ρv when comparing.
  • Inverse-square spreading assumes isotropic point-like source, negligible absorption/reflections, far from near-field effects.
  • Loudness is not determined by intensity ratio alone; avoid hearing-risk experiments and use hypothetical data.

Exit check

Two same-frequency waves have pressure peak-to-peak values 8 Pa and 12 Pa in one medium. Compare intensity.

Check reasoning

Second has 2.25 times the intensity. Peak amplitudes 4 and 6 Pa; squared ratio (6/4)².
