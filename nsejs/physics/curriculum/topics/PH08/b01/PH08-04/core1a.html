Frequency, period and wavelength

Phenomenon

A repeating sound pattern can be measured with a fixed microphone or a spatial snapshot; the two observations have different axes.

Scientific construction

A vibrating tuning fork can repeat its motion hundreds of times per second. When it drives air, neighbouring regions alternately compress and rarefy. At a fixed microphone, the pressure variation repeats in time; in a snapshot of the air, the compression pattern repeats in space. These are two different observations of one travelling disturbance. The particles themselves oscillate about equilibrium rather than accompanying the sound to the listener.

One cycle means return to the same state of oscillation, including direction of change. The period T is the time per cycle in seconds. Frequency f is the number of complete cycles per second, in hertz (Hz = s⁻¹). If N cycles occupy duration Δt, T=Δt/N and f=N/Δt=1/T. Count intervals between matching phase points: six successive peaks enclose five complete periods, not six. If an instrument labels the time axis in milliseconds, divide milliseconds by 1000 before computing hertz.

The wavelength λ is the shortest repeat distance between matching points of the spatial pattern, measured along propagation, in metres. In a sinusoidal pressure snapshot, successive compressions are a wavelength apart; a compression and its neighbouring rarefaction are half a wavelength apart. Likewise, two adjacent zero crossings normally have opposite slope and are half a period apart on a time trace. On an arbitrary periodic signal, identify a full repeating motif rather than assuming every peak or zero crossing is equivalent. A pressure graph is not a drawing of air particles moving up and down: its vertical axis denotes pressure variation.

To connect space and time, imagine following one compression in a uniform stationary medium. During one period the pattern moves one wavelength. Thus speed v=λ/T=fλ. The relation is kinematic: it measures the speed of the pattern, not the speed of the particles' oscillation. For the same steady source crossing a stationary boundary the temporal frequency remains fixed, while a changed propagation speed changes the wavelength. This principle will be developed further in PH08-05. Do not combine a time record from one wave with a spatial record of another just because both are sound.

A practical measurement uses several cycles to reduce the fractional error caused by locating a peak. For example, if each endpoint can be read only within 0.1 ms, the uncertainty in a time interval can be as large as 0.2 ms, whether it spans one or several cycles. Dividing a longer span by its cycle count can improve the period estimate provided that the frequency stays stable. Measurement resolution does not cure systematic clock error or uncertainty in the cycle count.

Build the model

  1. Choose whether the observation fixes position or time. Why: A fixed sensor measures temporal repetition; a snapshot measures spatial repetition.
  2. Locate same-phase points and count full intervals. Why: Complete cycles return to the same oscillation state; N full cycles occupy N periods or wavelengths.
  3. Convert units and calculate the reciprocal, then track a compression through one period. Why: A pattern traverses one wavelength in one period in a uniform medium; hence v=λ/T=fλ.
  4. Check phase and independent speed or interval data. Why: An independent measurement can reveal an incorrect cycle count or incompatible records.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Four successive crests on a time trace appear at 0, 2, 4 and 6 ms. Find T and f.

Three cycles span 6 ms, T=2 ms=0.002 s, f=500 Hz.

Independent check: 3/0.006=500 Hz.

CONNECTED

A pressure snapshot shows compression at 0.10 m and adjacent rarefaction at 0.45 m. The period is 2.0 ms. Find λ and v.

Half-wavelength is 0.35 m, λ=0.70 m; v=0.70/0.002=350 m/s.

Independent check: In one period the pressure pattern moves 0.70 m.

ADVANCED

Two successive compression maxima of a travelling wave are 0.90 m apart. A stationary source maintains 400 Hz when entering another medium with v=300 m/s. Predict the new λ and whether the frequency changes.

Initially v=360 m/s. At stationary boundary the source fixes f=400 Hz, new λ=300/400=0.75 m. The wavelength decreases, not the frequency.

Independent check: Check 400×0.75=300 m/s and compare the ratio of wavelengths with speed ratio.

Misconception and repair

Every neighbouring peak, zero crossing or compression–rarefaction pair is one complete cycle; a pressure graph shows the particle trajectory.

A pressure trace crosses zero going upward then downward 2 ms later. Does the same oscillation state recur? What does the vertical axis measure?

Track matching phase and axis quantity. Opposite-slope zero crossings of a sinusoid span half a period; pressure is not transverse particle displacement.

Limits

  • A single period and wavelength describe a periodic wave or a specified frequency component; irregular pulses need other descriptors.
  • Use stationary source/observer and a uniform stationary medium for the simple travelling-wave relation; do not apply it across different waves.
  • Measure the spacing between points at the same phase, not simply adjacent extrema or zero crossings.
  • Frequency comparisons between different media require identifying the source; v=fλ is not a claim that all sound travels at the same speed.

Exit check

Successive pressure minima arrive at a microphone 5 ms apart. At one instant a compression and its adjacent rarefaction are 0.825 m apart. Find f, λ and v.

Check reasoning

200 Hz, 1.65 m and 330 m/s. T=0.005 s; λ=2×0.825 m; v=1.65/0.005.
