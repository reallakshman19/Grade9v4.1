Sound speed: periodic waves and travel-time measurements

Phenomenon

A sound pulse takes measurable time to travel between sensors; a periodic source supplies an independent wavelength and frequency measurement.

Scientific construction

A sound disturbance transports energy through a medium. In a uniform stationary medium, a periodic pattern advances one wavelength λ during one period T, so its speed v=λ/T=fλ. This is a relation between the properties of the same wave, not a prescription for changing the speed by changing frequency. Sound speed depends on the medium and its state, including temperature. If a source maintains its frequency as a wave crosses a stationary boundary, the temporal repetitions remain continuous; wavelength changes when speed changes.

A pulse provides an independent speed measurement. For a known straight path length L and a measured propagation time t, v=L/t. A real trigger or sensor may add a fixed time offset τ, so a single reported time cannot separate τ from propagation. With the same apparatus at two different path lengths, t₂−t₁=(L₂−L₁)/v, so the slope of time versus distance is 1/v. The intercept is τ under the constant-offset model. A third observation can test that model rather than merely reproduce the two points that determined it. If the signal is reflected, the path length must include all propagation legs; PH08-08 develops that geometry.

If a direct path includes consecutive uniform sections, total travel time equals L₁/v₁+L₂/v₂. The effective speed is total distance divided by total travel time, not the arithmetic mean of speeds unless special conditions establish it. A slower segment contributes a disproportionately large travel time. At a stationary interface, frequency stays fixed for steady transmission; use the local wavelength to infer the local speed. For an unknown length fraction, separate the known total length from the unknown segment length, then write a time equation for each independent observation. Two measurements with the same unknown common delay may be redundant; subtracting them removes that delay only if they actually differ in propagation path.

A reliable calculation labels the horizontal axis, distinguishes pulse delay from oscillation period, converts milliseconds to seconds, and checks the inferred speed against an independent measurement. If the data cannot distinguish two models, report the ambiguity. This subtopic supports the existing Core2 sound-wave candidate; the timing and layered-medium inverse constructions are new authored transfer, not additional authenticated exam questions.

Build the model

  1. Identify a temporal period and same-phase spatial spacing or a pulse's physical path. Why: A complete period advances one wavelength; a pulse path is a separate measurement.
  2. Express travel time as segment lengths divided by local speeds. Why: Consecutive travel times add by definition; the speed is constant only within each stated segment.
  3. Remove common offset with independent differences and solve for unknown length or speed. Why: Subtracting cancels a genuinely common instrumental term; independent paths supply the missing constraint.
  4. Test the result on a measurement not used to fit the model. Why: Residuals reveal incorrect path, nonconstant delay or incompatible data.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A sound source has frequency 500 Hz and wavelength 0.68 m. Find the wave speed.

v=500×0.68=340 m/s.

Independent check: T=0.002 s; the pattern advances 0.68 m in that time.

CONNECTED

A direct pulse reaches a sensor at 0.68 m after 3 ms and at 1.70 m after 6 ms; apparatus delay is constant. Find v and τ.

Difference 1.02 m/0.003 s=340 m/s. τ=3−2=1 ms.

Independent check: At 1.70 m, 5 ms propagation plus 1 ms delay gives 6 ms.

ADVANCED

A 1.2 m path has 0.4 m at 200 m/s and 0.8 m at 400 m/s. Find propagation time and effective speed; source f=400 Hz.

t=2+2=4 ms, effective speed=300 m/s. Local wavelengths 0.5 m and 1 m.

Independent check: Distance = 200×0.002+400×0.002=1.2 m; arithmetic mean happens to equal the effective speed here because times match.

Misconception and repair

A higher frequency automatically means a higher speed; averaging medium speeds or including a trigger delay gives the physical speed.

Can two different frequencies travel with the same speed? What happens to the arrival-time slope when every timestamp is shifted?

Use local v=fλ, add propagation times, and fit the common offset separately.

Limits

  • Uniform stationary medium within each segment; fixed source frequency across a stationary interface.
  • Straight prescribed path, no refraction or reflections unless stated; pulse detection delay is additive and constant where assumed.
  • Time-of-flight and period are distinct time quantities; avoid applying v=fλ to mismatched observations.
  • Effective speed uses total path/total propagation time, excluding sensor delay.

Exit check

A 600 Hz source has wavelengths 0.50 m and 0.75 m in two media. A pulse crosses 0.6 m in each. Find transit time.

Check reasoning

3.33 ms (approximately). Speeds 300 and 450 m/s, times 2 ms and 1.333 ms; add them.
