PH08-01

Vibrating sources: excitation, frequency and coupling to air

Start with a vibrating source and the causal chain from excitation through the medium to the detector.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A vibrating ruler can remain visibly in motion while the sound reaching a listener becomes much weaker.

Scientific construction

A sound source is a mechanical system capable of transferring energy into its surroundings. A plucked string moves back and forth, a stretched membrane oscillates, and a vibrating reed or changing airflow can excite an air column. The disturbance moves through a material medium to a receiver. The material of the source is not carried to the listener. The source can keep vibrating even when coupling to the surrounding medium becomes weak.

A periodic source repeats its state after a period T; frequency f counts full repetitions per second. A sound that is more complicated than a sinusoid may contain multiple frequency components. A string, membrane and air column can have different mode shapes and may produce similar fundamental frequencies. Hearing a frequency does not uniquely identify an instrument or the mechanism by which it was excited. A string may be plucked, struck or bowed; similar periodic motion can result from different driving methods.

Separate the causal links: energy input causes source motion, the moving boundary excites a disturbance, the medium transports it, and the receiver converts it into a measured signal. A large source displacement need not imply a large pressure signal far away. Source area, geometry, coupling, intervening medium and detector response matter. A sensor attached to the source measures a different quantity from a microphone in air. A microphone voltage is not automatically a pressure amplitude without calibration.

To observe an oscillation, label displacement and time axes, count complete repetitions between matching phase points, and convert units. A source and receiver time trace may have the same repetition frequency with a delay if a single steady tone propagates through a stationary medium and the receiver response is suitable. A pulse envelope can establish arrival time; two periodic sinusoidal traces alone can have a phase delay ambiguous by whole periods. This distinction will be developed in PH08-04 and PH08-07. A quiet receiver signal could reflect weaker source vibration, changed coupling, propagation obstruction or a sensor fault. A controlled test keeps source motion and receiver conditions matched while varying one link.

For a source in a bell jar as air is removed, an ideal vacuum cannot carry sound through the gas path, yet mechanical vibration may continue and structure-borne sound may still travel through solid supports. Do not infer that the source stopped merely because an external microphone signal weakened. Use direct motion measurement and path controls. Avoid high sound levels in demonstrations. The current inspectable Core2 bank has no matching source-excitation question; these examples are original transfer.

Build the model

  1. Identify source, drive, medium and receiver. Why: They are distinct components of the causal chain.
  2. Measure source oscillation independently of receiver output. Why: A weak receiver signal alone does not establish weaker source motion.
  3. Count same-phase repetitions and identify the recorded variable. Why: Frequency is determined by repetition, not graph height.
  4. Control a single coupling or propagation link and test competing explanations. Why: Independent observations distinguish source change from path or sensor change.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A membrane completes 12 cycles in 0.06 s. Find frequency and period.

200 Hz; 0.005 s.

Independent check: 12×0.005=0.06 s.

CONNECTED

A string oscillates with unchanged measured period and displacement, but enclosing it reduces microphone output. Does this prove its frequency changed?

No. The source measurement is unchanged; enclosure may alter coupling or propagation. Check calibrated microphone and path.

Independent check: Compare source traces independently of receiver output.

ADVANCED

Two sensors record the same steady 250 Hz tone, with one trace apparently delayed 1 ms. Is travel delay unique?

Period 4 ms; delays 1 ms+4n ms can give the same periodic phase offset. A pulse or independent path length constrains n.

Independent check: Use a timed onset, not just steady phase.

Misconception and repair

A weaker sound at the listener means the source stopped vibrating; equal frequency identifies the same instrument.

Can a source vibration sensor remain unchanged while a microphone output falls?

Separate excitation, coupling, propagation and detection; use independent measurements.

Limits

  • Periodic source model only when repetition is stable; nonperiodic pulses require other descriptors.
  • Microphone voltage requires calibration before pressure comparisons.
  • Same-frequency claim assumes steady transmission and suitable detector; phase delay can be ambiguous.
  • A vacuum removes gas-borne sound but not necessarily solid-borne bypasses or electronic artifacts.

Exit check

A source completes five intervals between the first and sixth matching peaks in 20 ms. Find f.

Check reasoning

250 Hz. T=4 ms; f=1/0.004.

PH08-02

Longitudinal waves: displacement, compression and pressure graphs

Examine what actually moves in the medium. Relative particle displacements explain compression and rarefaction, not the shape of a drawn wave alone.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A compression can move along a row of air particles while each particle only oscillates around its equilibrium position.

Scientific construction

In a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate parallel to the direction of propagation. A compression is a region where particles are temporarily closer together than in their equilibrium arrangement; a rarefaction is a region where they are farther apart. In a sound wave, the disturbance and energy propagate through a medium while particles move locally. A diagram with a wavy line above a horizontal axis need not depict transverse particle motion: it may show the value of a longitudinal displacement or pressure field as a function of position.

Imagine equilibrium positions x₁ and x₂ separated by Δx. At a snapshot, their positions become x₁+ξ₁ and x₂+ξ₂. Their new separation is Δx+ξ₂−ξ₁. If the second displacement is smaller than the first, the spacing decreases and the region is compressed. If the second displacement is larger, the spacing increases and it is rarefied. If both displacements are equal, their separation is unchanged even if both have moved far from equilibrium. Thus a displacement maximum is not automatically a compression maximum.

For a small smooth displacement field ξ(x), the fractional change in spacing is approximately ∂ξ/∂x. Conservation of mass gives fractional density change approximately −∂ξ/∂x; in a linear acoustic model excess pressure is proportional to compression, p′=−B∂ξ/∂x, where B is the appropriate bulk modulus. These derivative relations are optional authored enrichment; the finite-neighbour argument is sufficient for qualitative reasoning. A sinusoidal displacement crest has locally zero gradient; compression peaks near the downward zero crossing and rarefaction near the upward crossing, given the stated coordinate convention. The sign of a displacement itself does not tell whether neighbouring particles are compressed.

A spatial graph at one time describes field values along x. A time trace at one fixed detector describes how one measured field varies with time. Their slopes have different units and meanings. In a progressive sinusoidal wave the time trace may be related to the spatial trace if direction and wave speed are known. Do not infer spatial compression directly from a temporal displacement slope without the travelling-wave model and direction. For a right-moving profile ξ(x−vt), the temporal derivative equals −v times the spatial derivative; reversing direction changes the sign relation.

When constructing a graph, label equilibrium coordinate, particle displacement or excess pressure, and physical units. Draw the actual positions of a few particles to check the inferred compression. In an inverse problem, a pressure snapshot constrains differences in displacement, not the common offset of every displacement; a boundary or displacement reference supplies missing information. A uniform offset added to all positions leaves spacing unchanged. This is a model ambiguity, not bulk transport by the sound wave. The existing Core2 wave snapshot uses displacement graphs, but no separate matching question has been established for this exact subtopic; the new constructions are authored transfer rather than direct exam evidence.

Build the model

  1. Mark equilibrium particle positions and their signed longitudinal displacements. Why: Actual position is equilibrium position plus displacement.
  2. Calculate changed adjacent spacing rather than compare displacement magnitudes. Why: Compression corresponds to reduced spacing and increased density.
  3. Translate spacing changes into spatial displacement slope and qualitative pressure. Why: Negative slope gives compression under the stated coordinate convention.
  4. Test inverse or temporal interpretation with direction and reference data. Why: Only spatial differences determine spacing; boundary and propagation information can resolve ambiguity.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Particles at 0 and 10 mm have displacements +1 and −1 mm. Find actual spacing.

8 mm, compression.

Independent check: Actual positions 1 and 9 mm.

CONNECTED

Four equilibrium positions 0,10,20,30 mm have displacements 0,+1,+1,0 mm. Classify intervals.

Actual 0,11,21,30; spacings 11,10,9 mm: rarefaction, unchanged, compression.

Independent check: Displacement maximum alone did not fix compression.

ADVANCED

A spatial displacement trace has equal upward and downward zero crossings. Where are pressure extrema in the ideal sinusoidal model?

Downward spatial crossing corresponds to compression/high pressure, upward crossing to rarefaction/low pressure.

Independent check: Use sign of −spatial slope, not sign of displacement.

Misconception and repair

A displacement crest is a compression crest, and the wavy graph is a transverse particle path.

If adjacent particles have the same positive displacement, has their separation decreased?

Calculate actual spacing; distinguish field value, spatial gradient and direction of physical particle motion.

Limits

  • Small longitudinal displacements and locally smooth field for derivative/linear pressure model.
  • Neighbour labels refer to equilibrium positions; displacements must not imply particle crossing.
  • Pressure and displacement traces are distinct variables; spatial and temporal slopes not interchangeable.
  • Ideal uniform offset ambiguity is a snapshot relation; physical boundary conditions and dynamics may constrain it.

Exit check

Equilibrium positions 0, 10, 20 mm have displacements +1, +2, 0 mm. Classify spacings.

Check reasoning

11 mm rarefaction, 8 mm compression. Actual positions 1,12,20 mm.

PH08-03

Sound propagation in media: paths, speed and bypasses

Now compare possible material paths and propagation mechanisms. A vibrating source and a detected sound are different observations.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate for this exact subtopic in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A tap on a long rail may reach a detector through both the solid rail and the surrounding air.

Scientific construction

Sound is a mechanical disturbance: it propagates because neighbouring portions of a material interact. In air and water, a local compression changes pressure and accelerates neighbouring fluid. In a solid, elastic deformation can transmit disturbances through the material. A vacuum provides no material gas path for an acoustic wave, although a source can still vibrate and mechanical energy can reach a detector through a solid mounting. Electromagnetic signals do not require a material medium and are not acoustic propagation through vacuum.

The propagation speed is not the speed of individual molecules or the source. In a simple fluid, a stronger restoring response tends to increase sound speed, while greater inertia tends to reduce it. The linear fluid expression v=√(B/ρ) illustrates the competition between bulk modulus and density; density alone cannot rank two arbitrary media. Typical sound speeds in water and many solids exceed the speed in air, but the material properties and wave mode determine the actual result. In solids, different wave modes and geometry can change the effective propagation speed; do not apply a fluid modulus formula indiscriminately.

A timed pulse can travel by more than one route. A tap may propagate along a rail and through nearby air. Each route has its own path length, wave speed, and possible coupling delay. The earliest arrival need not be the geometrically shortest route, because a longer path through a faster medium can arrive first. A detector's first arrival is therefore evidence about the minimum travel time among detectable paths, not automatically about the physical distance to the source.

If a pulse traverses successive uniform media, add the travel time in each segment: t=L₁/v₁+L₂/v₂. If paths are parallel alternatives, calculate their complete times separately and compare them, rather than add them. At a stationary interface a steady source's frequency is preserved, while speed and wavelength may change. A wave can also reflect or refract; a path specified in a problem assumes the stated route is physically available. A signal arriving at a detector does not prove that every geometrical path is open.

Use path diagrams and matched timing references. For two known routes, vary one segment or interrupt one path, then predict how the arrival sequence changes. Independent observations can distinguish a structure-borne path from a gas-borne path, but changing the source coupling or detector threshold may also change which pulses are visible. The inspectable Core2 bank contains a related wave snapshot elsewhere but no exact medium-path question for this subtopic; these are authored transfer.

Build the model

  1. Identify the source, receiver and possible material routes. Why: Each route must provide a physical propagation path.
  2. Determine whether route segments are sequential or alternative. Why: Sequential times add, while alternative arrivals are compared.
  3. Convert each actual path length to a travel time. Why: Wave speed relates disturbance distance to time in that medium.
  4. Compare predictions with interventions and detection evidence. Why: A missing pulse may reflect a blocked route or inadequate detection, not absence of all vibration.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A pulse travels 3 m in a medium at 1500 m/s. Find travel time.

2 ms.

Independent check: 3/1500=0.002 s.

CONNECTED

A rail path 12 m at 4000 m/s and air path 10.2 m at 340 m/s connect source and receiver. Which arrives first?

Rail 3 ms, air 30 ms; rail first despite longer path.

Independent check: Compare complete route times, not path lengths alone.

ADVANCED

A prescribed route crosses 1.5 m of liquid at 1500 m/s then 0.68 m air at 340 m/s. Find transit time.

1+2=3 ms.

Independent check: Total distance 2.18 m, effective speed≈726.7 m/s, not the arithmetic mean.

Misconception and repair

Sound travels faster solely in denser media; the first arrival always follows the shortest route.

Could a long rail path arrive before a shorter air path? Which material properties and route times matter?

Compare restoring response and inertia where applicable; trace and time each available physical path.

Limits

  • Mechanical acoustic waves require a material path; alternative solid or electronic paths may persist in vacuum experiments.
  • Uniform speed within each specified segment; path length is actual travelled length.
  • Arrival labels assume detectable pulses and matched timing origin; coupling delays must be stated or accounted for.
  • No universal speed ranking by density alone; distinguish fluid and solid wave models.

Exit check

A 4 m solid path at 2000 m/s and a 1.02 m air path at 340 m/s run in parallel. Which arrives first?

Check reasoning

Solid path: 2 ms versus air 3 ms. The shorter path is not the faster-time route.

PH08-04

Frequency, period and wavelength

With the physical path established, quantify repetition in space and time and connect those measurements only for the same travelling wave.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Related Core2 candidate (source/QRT HOLD). NSEJS-2024-P-Q34 uses wave quantities as a prerequisite. Complete the PH08-07 phase-translation construction and its Core1B before interpreting this related item. The bank labels it D2; its original graph is missing. Evidence.

Phenomenon

A repeating sound pattern can be measured with a fixed microphone or a spatial snapshot; the two observations have different axes.

Scientific construction

A vibrating tuning fork can repeat its motion hundreds of times per second. When it drives air, neighbouring regions alternately compress and rarefy. At a fixed microphone, the pressure variation repeats in time; in a snapshot of the air, the compression pattern repeats in space. These are two different observations of one travelling disturbance. The particles themselves oscillate about equilibrium rather than accompanying the sound to the listener.

One cycle means return to the same state of oscillation, including direction of change. The period T is the time per cycle in seconds. Frequency f is the number of complete cycles per second, in hertz (Hz = s⁻¹). If N cycles occupy duration Δt, T=Δt/N and f=N/Δt=1/T. Count intervals between matching phase points: six successive peaks enclose five complete periods, not six. If an instrument labels the time axis in milliseconds, divide milliseconds by 1000 before computing hertz.

The wavelength λ is the shortest repeat distance between matching points of the spatial pattern, measured along propagation, in metres. In a sinusoidal pressure snapshot, successive compressions are a wavelength apart; a compression and its neighbouring rarefaction are half a wavelength apart. Likewise, two adjacent zero crossings normally have opposite slope and are half a period apart on a time trace. On an arbitrary periodic signal, identify a full repeating motif rather than assuming every peak or zero crossing is equivalent. A pressure graph is not a drawing of air particles moving up and down: its vertical axis denotes pressure variation.

To connect space and time, imagine following one compression in a uniform stationary medium. During one period the pattern moves one wavelength. Thus speed v=λ/T=fλ. The relation is kinematic: it measures the speed of the pattern, not the speed of the particles' oscillation. For the same steady source crossing a stationary boundary the temporal frequency remains fixed, while a changed propagation speed changes the wavelength. This principle will be developed further in PH08-05. Do not combine a time record from one wave with a spatial record of another just because both are sound.

A practical measurement uses several cycles to reduce the fractional error caused by locating a peak. For example, if each endpoint can be read only within 0.1 ms, the uncertainty in a time interval can be as large as 0.2 ms, whether it spans one or several cycles. Dividing a longer span by its cycle count can improve the period estimate provided that the frequency stays stable. Measurement resolution does not cure systematic clock error or uncertainty in the cycle count.

Build the model

  1. Choose whether the observation fixes position or time. Why: A fixed sensor measures temporal repetition; a snapshot measures spatial repetition.
  2. Locate same-phase points and count full intervals. Why: Complete cycles return to the same oscillation state; N full cycles occupy N periods or wavelengths.
  3. Convert units and calculate the reciprocal, then track a compression through one period. Why: A pattern traverses one wavelength in one period in a uniform medium; hence v=λ/T=fλ.
  4. Check phase and independent speed or interval data. Why: An independent measurement can reveal an incorrect cycle count or incompatible records.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Four successive crests on a time trace appear at 0, 2, 4 and 6 ms. Find T and f.

Three cycles span 6 ms, T=2 ms=0.002 s, f=500 Hz.

Independent check: 3/0.006=500 Hz.

CONNECTED

A pressure snapshot shows compression at 0.10 m and adjacent rarefaction at 0.45 m. The period is 2.0 ms. Find λ and v.

Half-wavelength is 0.35 m, λ=0.70 m; v=0.70/0.002=350 m/s.

Independent check: In one period the pressure pattern moves 0.70 m.

ADVANCED

Two successive compression maxima of a travelling wave are 0.90 m apart. A stationary source maintains 400 Hz when entering another medium with v=300 m/s. Predict the new λ and whether the frequency changes.

Initially v=360 m/s. At stationary boundary the source fixes f=400 Hz, new λ=300/400=0.75 m. The wavelength decreases, not the frequency.

Independent check: Check 400×0.75=300 m/s and compare the ratio of wavelengths with speed ratio.

Misconception and repair

Every neighbouring peak, zero crossing or compression–rarefaction pair is one complete cycle; a pressure graph shows the particle trajectory.

A pressure trace crosses zero going upward then downward 2 ms later. Does the same oscillation state recur? What does the vertical axis measure?

Track matching phase and axis quantity. Opposite-slope zero crossings of a sinusoid span half a period; pressure is not transverse particle displacement.

Limits

  • A single period and wavelength describe a periodic wave or a specified frequency component; irregular pulses need other descriptors.
  • Use stationary source/observer and a uniform stationary medium for the simple travelling-wave relation; do not apply it across different waves.
  • Measure the spacing between points at the same phase, not simply adjacent extrema or zero crossings.
  • Frequency comparisons between different media require identifying the source; v=fλ is not a claim that all sound travels at the same speed.

Exit check

Successive pressure minima arrive at a microphone 5 ms apart. At one instant a compression and its adjacent rarefaction are 0.825 m apart. Find f, λ and v.

Check reasoning

200 Hz, 1.65 m and 330 m/s. T=0.005 s; λ=2×0.825 m; v=1.65/0.005.

PH08-05

Sound speed: periodic waves and travel-time measurements

Use the measured quantities to infer propagation speed and travel time, checking for unknown offsets and mixed media.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Supporting prerequisite · existing Core2 candidate · source/QRT HOLD. NSEJS-2024-P-Q34 (NSEJS-24-MECH-07). This candidate is producer-labelled D2, not admitted D3/D4 evidence. Original graph missing; Core2 commitment disabled. Pinned source identity · Primary related Core1A.

Phenomenon

A sound pulse takes measurable time to travel between sensors; a periodic source supplies an independent wavelength and frequency measurement.

Scientific construction

A sound disturbance transports energy through a medium. In a uniform stationary medium, a periodic pattern advances one wavelength λ during one period T, so its speed v=λ/T=fλ. This is a relation between the properties of the same wave, not a prescription for changing the speed by changing frequency. Sound speed depends on the medium and its state, including temperature. If a source maintains its frequency as a wave crosses a stationary boundary, the temporal repetitions remain continuous; wavelength changes when speed changes.

A pulse provides an independent speed measurement. For a known straight path length L and a measured propagation time t, v=L/t. A real trigger or sensor may add a fixed time offset τ, so a single reported time cannot separate τ from propagation. With the same apparatus at two different path lengths, t₂−t₁=(L₂−L₁)/v, so the slope of time versus distance is 1/v. The intercept is τ under the constant-offset model. A third observation can test that model rather than merely reproduce the two points that determined it. If the signal is reflected, the path length must include all propagation legs; PH08-08 develops that geometry.

If a direct path includes consecutive uniform sections, total travel time equals L₁/v₁+L₂/v₂. The effective speed is total distance divided by total travel time, not the arithmetic mean of speeds unless special conditions establish it. A slower segment contributes a disproportionately large travel time. At a stationary interface, frequency stays fixed for steady transmission; use the local wavelength to infer the local speed. For an unknown length fraction, separate the known total length from the unknown segment length, then write a time equation for each independent observation. Two measurements with the same unknown common delay may be redundant; subtracting them removes that delay only if they actually differ in propagation path.

A reliable calculation labels the horizontal axis, distinguishes pulse delay from oscillation period, converts milliseconds to seconds, and checks the inferred speed against an independent measurement. If the data cannot distinguish two models, report the ambiguity. This subtopic supports the existing Core2 sound-wave candidate; the timing and layered-medium inverse constructions are new authored transfer, not additional authenticated exam questions.

Build the model

  1. Identify a temporal period and same-phase spatial spacing or a pulse's physical path. Why: A complete period advances one wavelength; a pulse path is a separate measurement.
  2. Express travel time as segment lengths divided by local speeds. Why: Consecutive travel times add by definition; the speed is constant only within each stated segment.
  3. Remove common offset with independent differences and solve for unknown length or speed. Why: Subtracting cancels a genuinely common instrumental term; independent paths supply the missing constraint.
  4. Test the result on a measurement not used to fit the model. Why: Residuals reveal incorrect path, nonconstant delay or incompatible data.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A sound source has frequency 500 Hz and wavelength 0.68 m. Find the wave speed.

v=500×0.68=340 m/s.

Independent check: T=0.002 s; the pattern advances 0.68 m in that time.

CONNECTED

A direct pulse reaches a sensor at 0.68 m after 3 ms and at 1.70 m after 6 ms; apparatus delay is constant. Find v and τ.

Difference 1.02 m/0.003 s=340 m/s. τ=3−2=1 ms.

Independent check: At 1.70 m, 5 ms propagation plus 1 ms delay gives 6 ms.

ADVANCED

A 1.2 m path has 0.4 m at 200 m/s and 0.8 m at 400 m/s. Find propagation time and effective speed; source f=400 Hz.

t=2+2=4 ms, effective speed=300 m/s. Local wavelengths 0.5 m and 1 m.

Independent check: Distance = 200×0.002+400×0.002=1.2 m; arithmetic mean happens to equal the effective speed here because times match.

Misconception and repair

A higher frequency automatically means a higher speed; averaging medium speeds or including a trigger delay gives the physical speed.

Can two different frequencies travel with the same speed? What happens to the arrival-time slope when every timestamp is shifted?

Use local v=fλ, add propagation times, and fit the common offset separately.

Limits

  • Uniform stationary medium within each segment; fixed source frequency across a stationary interface.
  • Straight prescribed path, no refraction or reflections unless stated; pulse detection delay is additive and constant where assumed.
  • Time-of-flight and period are distinct time quantities; avoid applying v=fλ to mismatched observations.
  • Effective speed uses total path/total propagation time, excluding sensor delay.

Exit check

A 600 Hz source has wavelengths 0.50 m and 0.75 m in two media. A pulse crosses 0.6 m in each. Find transit time.

Check reasoning

3.33 ms (approximately). Speeds 300 and 450 m/s, times 2 ms and 1.333 ms; add them.

PH08-06

Pitch, loudness, amplitude and intensity

A propagation speed is not a measure of pitch or loudness. Separate frequency, pressure amplitude, intensity and perception.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Supporting prerequisite · existing Core2 candidate · source/QRT HOLD. NSEJS-2024-P-Q34 (NSEJS-24-MECH-07). This candidate is producer-labelled D2, not admitted D3/D4 evidence. Original graph missing; Core2 commitment disabled. Pinned source identity · Primary related Core1A.

Phenomenon

Two sounds can have the same pitch but different measured amplitude and perceived loudness.

Scientific construction

A vibrating source produces an oscillation; a sensor may record displacement, pressure, or an electrical signal proportional to one of them. In a symmetric sinusoid, amplitude is the maximum deviation from the equilibrium value, half the peak-to-peak span. A graph's horizontal repeat interval determines period and frequency. A tall graph does not imply a higher frequency. Pitch describes perception of frequency (especially the fundamental of a simple tone), whereas loudness depends on intensity, frequency sensitivity, duration, listening conditions and the listener.

Sound intensity is the average power transported per unit area normal to propagation. For a linear progressive sinusoidal plane wave in the same medium, mean intensity is I=p_rms²/(ρv), where p_rms is the root-mean-square excess pressure, ρ is density and v is sound speed. For a sinusoid p_rms=p_peak/√2. Thus two matched pressure measurements in the same medium have intensity ratio (p₁/p₂)². Pressure and displacement amplitudes are not interchangeable. In the same medium a displacement amplitude A gives pressure amplitude proportional to fA for a linear travelling sinusoid; comparing different frequencies by displacement amplitude alone is insufficient. The latter relations are explicitly enrichment, not asserted compulsory.

A separate geometric model applies to an ideal isotropic source in an unbounded lossless medium: power crosses a spherical area 4πr², so intensity P/(4πr²). That is an inverse-square relation, not a rule that all spaces or sources obey. Under those conditions and a fixed medium, pressure amplitude is proportional to 1/r. In rooms, reflections, directivity, absorption or interference can defeat this model. If two different-frequency tones are averaged over sufficiently long intervals, their mean intensities may be added when cross terms average out; coherent same-frequency waves cannot always be treated that way.

The reasoning job is to decide what the sensor measured, separate frequency from amplitude, and select a propagation model supported by the conditions. A ratio of pressures is not a ratio of powers. A ratio of intensities does not establish the ratio of perceived loudness. The existing Core2 sound-wave candidate requires interpreting displacement amplitude; this unit prepares that prerequisite and extends it with original advanced intensity comparisons.

Build the model

  1. Read the graph's vertical physical quantity and equilibrium baseline. Why: Amplitude must describe a specified field; pressure, displacement and voltage are different quantities.
  2. Recover period from matching phase and separate it from amplitude. Why: Frequency uses temporal repetition, not vertical height.
  3. Select pressure-intensity or geometric spreading model under its explicit conditions. Why: Energy transported per area and wave impedance govern the pressure relation; conservation through spheres gives ideal spreading.
  4. Check a second observation or identify missing perceptual/medium information. Why: A numerical intensity does not uniquely establish perceived loudness.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A sinusoidal pressure trace has extrema +3 Pa and −3 Pa with a period of 5 ms. Find peak pressure and f.

Peak pressure 3 Pa, f=200 Hz.

Independent check: Peak-to-peak is 6 Pa; the full cycle repeats five milliseconds later.

CONNECTED

Two sinusoidal waves in the same medium have peak pressures 2 and 6 Pa. Compare their intensities.

I₂/I₁=(6/2)²=9.

Independent check: RMS pressure ratio is also 3, so square gives 9.

ADVANCED

An ideal isotropic source gives intensity 0.08 W/m² at 2 m. Find intensity at 4 m and pressure-amplitude ratio.

I(4)=0.02 W/m²; p(4)/p(2)=1/2 in the same medium.

Independent check: Intensity multiplied by spherical area is constant at both locations.

Misconception and repair

Taller wave graphs have higher pitch; twice the pressure amplitude means twice the intensity or twice the perceived loudness.

Compare two equal-period pressure traces with different amplitudes. Which measurements alone are enough to rank frequency, intensity and perception?

Read independent graph dimensions, square pressure ratio under matched impedance and keep perceptual claims conditional.

Limits

  • Sinusoidal signals and calibrated linear sensors for peak-to-peak inference; use the appropriate zero/reference level.
  • Pressure squared intensity relation assumes a progressive linear wave and common impedance ρv when comparing.
  • Inverse-square spreading assumes isotropic point-like source, negligible absorption/reflections, far from near-field effects.

PH08-07

Wave graphs: signed phase translation and inverse timing

Bring spatial and temporal representations together to reconstruct motion, retaining periodic ambiguity and measurement limits.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Related existing Core2 candidate — source/QRT HOLD. NSEJS-2024-P-Q34 (source ID NSEJS-24-MECH-07). Candidate bank labels it D2; the original graph and independent admission remain unresolved, and its reader disables commitment. This lesson develops original D3/D4 transfer, not an authenticated examination solution. Exact revision and coverage evidence.

Phenomenon

A travelling periodic profile may look unchanged at a later time although energy propagates.

Scientific construction

A snapshot of a travelling longitudinal sound wave can display each particle's signed displacement from equilibrium against its equilibrium position x. The vertical coordinate is a graph value, not the physical vertical position of the particle. A pressure snapshot instead records excess pressure. They are not the same physical quantity and their extrema need not coincide. Both may be periodic in the simple sinusoidal model; select the variable before interpreting any graph. The amplitude of a displacement wave is its maximum absolute displacement from equilibrium. Peak-to-peak distance on the vertical scale is twice that amplitude. Horizontal spacing of matching phase points determines wavelength; a crest and neighbouring trough are half a wavelength apart.

A useful way to reason is to imagine a transparent tracing of the snapshot. If a pattern moves right at constant speed v, after time t the tracing has shifted right by vt; if the signed velocity u is negative, shift left. The field value at a fixed coordinate x is the value originally at x−ut. Thus D(x,t)=D(x−ut,0). This is a statement about propagation of a pattern; one parcel of air does not travel at the wave speed. For example, a wave pattern can move metres while the local displacement amplitude is millimetres. In a sinusoidal profile, a crest initially at x0 has equivalent crests at x0+nλ, where n is any integer; after t their coordinates are x0+ut+nλ. This construction separates the travelling pattern from local oscillation.

An integer-wavelength translation reproduces the same graph at every coordinate. That does not mean the wave stopped. A half-wavelength shift changes a sinusoid's sign; a quarter-wavelength shift moves zero crossings to extrema. If a right-moving wave advances 1.5 wavelengths, the observable snapshot shift can be represented by half a wavelength, although the actual propagation distance was larger. A finite isolated pulse does not have this periodic ambiguity: its unique labelled feature can be followed directly.

The inverse problem is harder. Two snapshots showing equivalent phase markers at x1 and x2 give possible signed shifts x2−x1+nλ, not necessarily the smallest visible separation. For a time interval Δt, possible velocities are (x2−x1+nλ)/Δt. A declared speed range excludes candidates that violate it. An independent third snapshot at a different time tests the remaining candidates: compute where the same phase set would be and compare with the observation modulo λ. If more than one remains, report the ambiguity instead of guessing. A fixed detector's known period or an independently measured direction may also constrain the solution. Measurement uncertainty broadens the possible ranges and may prevent unique identification. These inverse constructions extend, rather than copy, a currently available Core2 wave-snapshot candidate.

In a laboratory, label axes and units, identify same-phase features, record propagation direction if known, and distinguish measured intervals from assumed numbers of cycles. A complete solution checks the sign of translation, units of distance and time, and the relevant periodic boundary. An independent check uses another phase marker or time observation, not repetition of the same arithmetic.

Build the model

  1. Identify field and same-phase spatial interval. Why: The graph axes distinguish longitudinal displacement from physical geometry; repeated phase establishes λ.
  2. Translate all phase families by the signed pattern shift uΔt. Why: A travelling field is D(x,t)=D(x−ut,0), not a particle trajectory.
  3. For an inverse problem enumerate shifts differing by nλ and apply the speed range. Why: Periodic repetition makes all those shifts observationally equivalent in two snapshots.
  4. Test remaining candidates against an independent observation and report nonuniqueness if necessary. Why: An additional time or phase constraint may reject aliases; it does not guarantee uniqueness.

Limits

  • A stationary sensor's time trace and a spatial snapshot are not interchangeable; no bulk particle transport is inferred.
  • Phase shift convention: positive signed shift is toward increasing x. Only a uniform repeating pattern returns after whole wavelengths.

PH08-08

Reflection and echo timing: geometry before formulas

A reflected path gives another travel-time observation. Identify geometry and the timing reference before using an echo formula.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Phenomenon

An echo delay measures a reflected propagation path; the distance to a reflector depends on the source and receiver geometry.

Scientific construction

Sound reflects when a wave meets a suitable boundary. A short pulse can arrive directly at a receiver and later along a reflected path. The echo is not a new sound emitted by the reflector; it is a returned part of the disturbance. A large smooth wall can be modelled as a specular reflector when its dimensions and surface conditions are appropriate. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection for the ideal ray model; roughness, diffraction and absorption can weaken or spread the pulse.

For a stationary source and receiver at the same location facing a planar wall, a normal reflected path goes a distance d to the wall and d back. The elapsed time since emission is 2d/v, so d=vt/2. This is not a universal echo formula. A receiver in another location sees a total reflected path equal to source-to-reflection-point plus reflection-point-to-receiver. If the recorded time is measured from the direct arrival rather than emission, add the direct travel time before inferring reflected length. In particular, the difference between two arrival times is a path-length difference, not the whole reflected path.

A plane reflection can be analysed using a virtual image of the source behind the wall. Reflect the source across the wall; a straight segment from this image to the real receiver has length equal to the two-leg reflected path. Where the segment crosses the wall determines the reflection point, provided the wall is sufficiently large. For source and receiver the same perpendicular distance d from the wall and separation b parallel to it, the reflected path is sqrt((2d)^2+b^2). The direct path is b. If excess delay Δt is measured, reflected length is b+vΔt. Solve for d only if the given geometry supports it. This geometric extension is taught as authored advanced reasoning.

If source and receiver are at different normal distances d_s and d_m on the same side, with separation b parallel to the wall, the image construction gives reflected length sqrt((d_s+d_m)^2+b^2) and direct length sqrt((d_s−d_m)^2+b^2). Equal measured lengths or delays can fit more than one arrangement when position information is missing. Draw the source, receiver, wall and timing reference before choosing an equation; then check the path actually reaches the reflector and the inferred geometry is physically valid.

An observer can distinguish two arrivals when the pulse duration, detector resolution and separation are appropriate. Human echo perception is not a universal exact timing threshold. The simple relations assume stationary objects and uniform propagation speed. Multiple reflection and moving geometries require additional path modelling, addressed in PH08-12.   1. Draw source, receiver, wall and timing origin. Why: Travel time corresponds to path length from emission, not a label on a diagram.

  3. Convert measured delay into total path or path difference as appropriate. Why: A direct-arrival reference subtracts the direct transit time.
  4. Solve the geometric constraint and check admissible distances and observation limits. Why: Nonnegative lengths and adequate wall extent are required; missing geometry may make the inference nonunique.

Echo and reverberation: arrival overlap and detectability

Extend reflection reasoning to finite-duration signals and detection thresholds: multiple returns can merge or disappear from the record.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Phenomenon

A short clap may produce a distinct repeat in one room but a long blended sound in another.

Scientific construction

When a source produces a short pulse in a room, part of the sound arrives directly and parts reflect from surfaces. A sufficiently delayed detectable return may be heard as a distinct echo. Numerous overlapping reflected arrivals can prolong the sound after the source stops; this persistence is reverberation. The distinction depends on the source signal, the listener or detector, the strength of returns and their timing. The familiar school estimate of a tenth of a second for a separately perceived echo is a rough contextual guideline, not an exact universal rule.

Draw a time axis beginning at emission. A direct arrival has a travel time L_direct/v; a reflected arrival has L_ref/v. The difference is the excess delay, not necessarily the full return time. A pulse of duration τ occupies an interval from its leading arrival t to t+τ if propagation does not substantially change its shape. Two finite pulses overlap if their intervals intersect; even nonoverlapping intervals may not be distinguishable if the detector needs a minimum quiet gap or a return is too weak. For a specified model with required quiet gap g, successive nonoverlapping pulses are separately resolved only when their leading times differ by at least τ+g. This is a stipulated detector rule, not a claim about all human hearing.

An example: pulses of duration 12 ms start at 0, 8 and 40 ms relative to the direct arrival. The first two overlap from 8 to12 ms and together occupy 0–20 ms; the third begins 20 ms after that cluster ends. If all are detectable and the specified quiet-gap criterion is 5 ms, the third is separable from the earlier cluster. A pulse starting only 3 ms after the cluster ends would not be separately resolved by that rule even though it does not overlap. In analysing a series of reflections, update the cluster end to the latest end of any pulse that overlaps or lies within the specified quiet-gap threshold.

Absorbent wall treatments can reduce reflected amplitudes and late energy, usually reducing reverberation. They do not necessarily change path travel times significantly when geometry and medium stay fixed. A weak reflection can be physically present but absent from the detector's record. Moving the source, changing a wall, or changing pulse duration can change overlap and perceived clarity without necessarily altering every path. Avoid using loud sounds in demonstrations; reason from recorded or hypothetical data.

For advanced tasks, compare two proposed room or detector models by predicting arrival intervals and thresholded observations, then use an independent change of duration or sensitivity to eliminate alternatives. Distinguish an observed unresolved cluster from a claim that there is only one path.   1. Identify direct and reflected leading times, pulse duration and detection threshold. Why: Arrival geometry and instrument response are separate inputs.

  3. Apply the stated amplitude and resolution rules to predicted records. Why: Detectability depends on both signal strength and temporal separation.
  4. Test a changed source duration or detector threshold against rival path models. Why: An independent observation can distinguish previously unresolved returns.

Echolocation uses emitted sound and reflected signals to infer something about the surroundings. Phenomenon

Two instruments can play the same note yet sound different, and a modified instrument may change pitch or loudness independently.

Scientific construction

A musical instrument is a system that excites mechanical vibration and couples it to the surrounding medium. A string instrument can be plucked or bowed; a membrane can be struck; a reed or air jet can excite an air column. The vibrating object and the air that carries sound to the listener are not the same thing. A sounding board may couple a vibrating string more effectively to air, changing radiated sound without requiring the fundamental frequency to change.

Pitch is associated mainly with repetition frequency or fundamental frequency for a simple periodic tone. Timbre describes differences in sound quality arising in part from the relative strengths of frequency components, their evolution in time and the attack/decay envelope. Two instruments may produce the same fundamental frequency but different overtones and envelopes; a listener may distinguish them even at similar pitch and loudness. A microphone spectrum is an instrument-dependent measurement: compare calibrated or consistently normalized data before attributing amplitude differences to the source. Loudness is a perceptual quantity, not directly a spectral peak height.

PH08-12

Multiple reflecting paths and inverse echo-sequence reconstruction

Combine the reflected-path model with multiple wall encounters; reconstruct candidate geometries without treating peak count as path count.

Dedicated reading · Dedicated reasoning practice

Core2 coverage gap. No matching candidate in the inspectable existing Physics bank. All tasks are authored transfer; coverage register. No direct exam claim.

Phenomenon

A single emitted pulse can generate several returns; distinct reflection orders can have identical arrival times.

Scientific construction

Imagine two stationary parallel walls and a pulse source with a receiver at the same point between them. Treat only the normal one-dimensional paths. Let the left distance be a and the right distance b, so wall separation L=a+b. A pulse emitted in both directions can return after one left-wall reflection or one right-wall reflection. Their lengths are 2a and 2b. When a=b, these returns coincide in time, so counting peaks does not count distinct paths.

The pulse can also continue beyond the receiver after its first pass and reflect from the opposite wall. A left-then-right reflection path travels a to the left wall, L to the right wall, then b back to the receiver; length a+L+b=2L. A right-then-left path has the same total length. Thus two distinct two-reflection paths have identical arrival times. For three reflections alternating left-right-left, length a+2L+a=2L+2a; the other order gives 2L+2b. These results assume that every relevant pulse continues propagating, reflecting and passing through the receiver without interruption. In practice weaker or overlapping echoes may be missing.

The time ordering provides information about geometry. If the two first-order arrivals are correctly identified at t_a and t_b, the common second-order arrival is predicted at t_a+t_b, because 2L=2a+2b. A third-order return is at t_a+t_b+t_a or t_a+t_b+t_b. The ordering of all peaks is not equivalent to simply numbering echoes, especially when two paths coincide or an instrument misses a pulse. A timing record alone may identify the unordered pair {a,b} but not which side is left. An apparent single first-order peak can be caused by coincident returns, missing data or a weak reflector; each assumption leads to a different inverse problem.

To resolve a mirror ambiguity, move the colocated emitter/receiver a known distance δ to the right while keeping walls fixed. Then a′=a+δ and b′=b−δ, so L remains constant. The one-reflection times change by ±2δ/v, whereas the common two-reflection arrival stays unchanged. An independent trace therefore tests both the assignment of sides and the total wall separation. If the detected peaks can be identified only as a subset of orders, record which assignments remain compatible rather than assume all peaks are present. Under the ideal model, a labelled higher-order arrival or a deliberately chosen displacement can eliminate alternatives.

This is an authored advanced extension of reflection and path timing. A graph or table of arrivals must distinguish elapsed time since emission, path multiplicity, and detection availability. Do not infer reflectivity or amplitude from arrival time alone; reflection coefficients, energy losses and interference require additional evidence.   1. Draw wall positions and trace each reflection order leg by leg. Why: Total path is the sum of travelled segments, not the number of reflections times a fixed distance.
  2. Group equal path lengths and convert each to an arrival time. Why: Two different paths may give a single observed arrival.
  3. Use independent order or displacement observations to constrain candidate geometry. Why: One-return times determine unordered distances; known displacement changes them oppositely while width is invariant.
  4. Check all observed arrivals and missing/overlapping possibilities. Why: A solution must fit the stated detection assumptions and cannot claim uniqueness from redundant data.

Independent check: Third paths 20 and28 m, each divided by 400.

ADVANCED

Identified first-order times are 20 and30 ms at v=300 m/s. Source shifts 1 m right; new first-order times 23.333 and26.667 ms. Identify sides.

Original distances 3 and4.5 m. If a=3,b=4.5, shifted distances 4,3.5 giving 26.667,23.333 ms. If a=4.5,b=3, shifted 5.5,2 giving 36.667,13.333 ms. First geometry fits; common second arrival remains 50 ms.

Independent check: Check width 7.5 m remains unchanged.

Misconception and repair

Each peak corresponds to exactly one reflection order and the first visible peak must identify the nearest wall.

Two-reflection paths left-right and right-left have equal lengths. Can a single peak establish only one path?

Enumerate path families and use additional observations to distinguish geometry; keep missing or overlapping returns explicit.

Limits

  • One-dimensional normal paths between stationary parallel walls, source/receiver colocated, uniform speed, no additional reflectors.
  • Assume path orders and missing detections only as explicitly given; coincidence does not imply one path.
  • Move source/receiver within the same walls; the total width remains constant.
  • Arrival times alone do not specify amplitude, phase interference or detectability; pulse duration and thresholds matter.

40 ms; 55 and65 ms. Sum first times for common second; add each first time for third.

Phenomenon

A moving source can change the frequency received by a stationary observer, while a stationary resonator selects particular vibration modes.

Scientific construction

EXTENSION — quantitative Doppler effect, standing waves and resonance are higher-level comparison material, not established compulsory NSEJS content. The Doppler effect is a change in observed frequency when source and observer motion alter the rate of arriving wavefronts. Work in the rest frame of a stationary medium. A source emitting at frequency f_s emits successive wavefronts separated in time by T_s=1/f_s. During that period a previous wavefront advances vT_s; if the source moves toward a stationary observer by u_sT_s, the next wavefront starts closer to the previous one. Ahead of a subsonic source, their spacing is (v−u_s)T_s. A stationary observer sees wavefronts pass at speed v, so f_obs=vf_s/(v−u_s). Behind the source the spacing is (v+u_s)T_s and the observed frequency is lower. These expressions assume uniform straight motion and a stationary medium, with source speed below wave speed.

A moving observer with stationary source is a different model. The emitted spacing remains v/f_s in the medium; an observer moving toward the wavefronts meets them at a greater relative speed, giving f_obs=(v+u_o)f_s/v for that specified geometry. Do not treat source and observer speeds as interchangeable in all acoustic cases. If motion directions or medium flow differ, derive the arrival rate using a consistent frame. Amplitude or intensity changes alone do not establish Doppler frequency shifts.

Standing waves arise from superposition of oppositely travelling waves of the same frequency under suitable boundary conditions. In an ideal fixed-end string, displacement nodes are at both ends. Allowed wavelengths satisfy L=nλ/2, giving f_n=nv/(2L). In an ideal open-open air column, displacement antinodes occur near both open ends, with the same frequency sequence in the simple model. In an ideal closed-open air column, displacement node at the closed end and antinode at the open end give L=(2n−1)λ/4, so only odd multiples of the lowest frequency occur. Pressure boundary descriptions are complementary; do not mix pressure nodes with displacement nodes. Real air columns have end corrections and losses.

Resonance occurs when periodic forcing excites a mode efficiently near its natural frequency. Damping affects the strength and width of the response; the driving frequency determines steady forced oscillation frequency in a linear model. A large amplitude need not prove a particular mode unless the mode shape, boundary conditions and excitation are known. If only some spectral peaks are visible, missing modes may be weakly excited or below threshold rather than forbidden. A mode identification problem must compare complete candidate frequency sets and additional mode-shape evidence.

Advanced reasoning connects frequency shifts with mode selection without conflating the two mechanisms. A moving source can deliver a shifted forcing frequency to a stationary resonator. Determine wavefront spacing first, then compare the received frequency with the possible modes of the resonator. A stationary instrument with a changed length can produce a different natural frequency without any Doppler shift. Independent source motion or boundary evidence distinguishes these models. The tasks use explicitly supplied ideal formulas; no exact matching Core2 question was found in the inspected bank.

Build the model

  1. Draw successive emissions and motion relative to the medium. Why: Source motion changes front spacing; observer motion changes encounter rate.
  2. Compute received frequency using the appropriate wavefront model. Why: Do not apply moving-source and moving-observer expressions interchangeably.
  3. Draw displacement nodes/antinodes and derive allowed wavelength family. Why: Boundary type determines half-wave versus odd-quarter-wave condition.
  4. Compare received forcing with mode predictions and check independent evidence. Why: A frequency match is conditional evidence of resonance, not unique identification of all properties.

Misconception and repair

Source and observer motion produce identical formulas, every tube allows every harmonic, and every large signal proves resonance.

Does a moving observer change the spacing emitted into stationary air? Which end of a closed-open tube is a displacement node?
