String-to-air coupling: frequency, wavelength, intensity and loudness

Scientific construction

An ideal stretched string supports transverse displacement waves. It can drive air through its motion and a sounding body, generating longitudinal pressure disturbances. At a stationary coupling boundary driven periodically, the transmitted tone can share the source frequency while its wavelength changes because λ=v/f and wave speeds in string and air differ. The string displacement amplitude is not the airborne pressure amplitude. In a linear progressive sinusoidal acoustic field of fixed impedance Z, time-averaged intensity is p_peak²/(2Z); if pressure amplitude doubles at unchanged Z, intensity quadruples. The coupling from string displacement to pressure depends on frequency, geometry, damping, direction and radiation. Perceived loudness is not a universal linear function of intensity. A school-level amplitude-squared statement needs an explicitly limited physical model; keep publisher key unchanged and distinguish measured quantities.

Worked foundation

A string supports a 200 Hz wave at100 m/s; airborne tone has speed340 m/s. Compare wavelengths.

String 0.5 m; air1.7 m. Common frequency does not make wavelengths equal.

Worked reasoning construction

Two sources have same measured string displacement amplitude but one radiates twice the pressure amplitude at a matched sensor. Can equal string amplitude prove equal intensity?

No; different coupling gives different pressure. At same impedance, intensity ratio4, but loudness not fixed.

Model limits and misconception

Only specified ideal/matched conditions; extension relations are supplied, not independently warranted compulsory syllabus scope. Identify confusion between source displacement, sound pressure, intensity and loudness, or between shared frequency and wavelength. Label each medium and measured variable, calculate λ separately, compare calibrated intensities only under stated impedance and field assumptions.
