Phenomenon

A single emitted pulse can generate several returns; distinct reflection orders can have identical arrival times.

Scientific construction

Imagine two stationary parallel walls and a pulse source with a receiver at the same point between them. Treat only the normal one-dimensional paths. Let the left distance be a and the right distance b, so wall separation L=a+b. A pulse emitted in both directions can return after one left-wall reflection or one right-wall reflection. Their lengths are 2a and 2b. When a=b, these returns coincide in time, so counting peaks does not count distinct paths.

The pulse can also continue beyond the receiver after its first pass and reflect from the opposite wall. A left-then-right reflection path travels a to the left wall, L to the right wall, then b back to the receiver; length a+L+b=2L. A right-then-left path has the same total length. Thus two distinct two-reflection paths have identical arrival times. For three reflections alternating left-right-left, length a+2L+a=2L+2a; the other order gives 2L+2b. These results assume that every relevant pulse continues propagating, reflecting and passing through the receiver without interruption. In practice weaker or overlapping echoes may be missing.

The time ordering provides information about geometry. If the two first-order arrivals are correctly identified at t_a and t_b, the common second-order arrival is predicted at t_a+t_b, because 2L=2a+2b. A third-order return is at t_a+t_b+t_a or t_a+t_b+t_b. The ordering of all peaks is not equivalent to simply numbering echoes, especially when two paths coincide or an instrument misses a pulse. A timing record alone may identify the unordered pair {a,b} but not which side is left. An apparent single first-order peak can be caused by coincident returns, missing data or a weak reflector; each assumption leads to a different inverse problem.

To resolve a mirror ambiguity, move the colocated emitter/receiver a known distance δ to the right while keeping walls fixed. Then a′=a+δ and b′=b−δ, so L remains constant. The one-reflection times change by ±2δ/v, whereas the common two-reflection arrival stays unchanged. An independent trace therefore tests both the assignment of sides and the total wall separation. If the detected peaks can be identified only as a subset of orders, record which assignments remain compatible rather than assume all peaks are present. Under the ideal model, a labelled higher-order arrival or a deliberately chosen displacement can eliminate alternatives.

Do not infer reflectivity or amplitude from arrival time alone; reflection coefficients, energy losses and interference require additional evidence. Build the model

  1. Draw wall positions and trace each reflection order leg by leg. Why: Total path is the sum of travelled segments, not the number of reflections times a fixed distance.
  2. Group equal path lengths and convert each to an arrival time. Why: Two different paths may give a single observed arrival.
  3. Use independent order or displacement observations to constrain candidate geometry. Why: One-return times determine unordered distances; known displacement changes them oppositely while width is invariant.
  4. Check all observed arrivals and missing/overlapping possibilities. Why: A solution must fit the stated detection assumptions and cannot claim uniqueness from redundant data.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

a=3 m, b=5 m, v=320 m/s. Find first and common second arrival times.

First 18.75 and31.25 ms; second 50 ms.

Independent check: Their sum is 50 ms; both double-reflection orders coincide.

CONNECTED

a=2 m, b=6 m, v=400 m/s. Find third-order times and compare with second.

L=8 m. Second 40 ms. Third 50 and70 ms.

Independent check: Third paths 20 and28 m, each divided by 400.

ADVANCED

Identified first-order times are 20 and30 ms at v=300 m/s. Source shifts 1 m right; new first-order times 23.333 and26.667 ms. Identify sides.

Original distances 3 and4.5 m. If a=3,b=4.5, shifted distances 4,3.5 giving 26.667,23.333 ms. If a=4.5,b=3, shifted 5.5,2 giving 36.667,13.333 ms. First geometry fits; common second arrival remains 50 ms.

Independent check: Check width 7.5 m remains unchanged.

Misconception and repair

Each peak corresponds to exactly one reflection order and the first visible peak must identify the nearest wall.

Two-reflection paths left-right and right-left have equal lengths. Can a single peak establish only one path?

Enumerate path families and use additional observations to distinguish geometry; keep missing or overlapping returns explicit.

Limits

  • One-dimensional normal paths between stationary parallel walls, source/receiver colocated, uniform speed, no additional reflectors.
  • Assume path orders and missing detections only as explicitly given; coincidence does not imply one path.
  • Move source/receiver within the same walls; the total width remains constant.
  • Arrival times alone do not specify amplitude, phase interference or detectability; pulse duration and thresholds matter.

Exit check

First-order echoes are 15 and25 ms from a source between two walls. What is the common second-order time and the possible third-order times?

40 ms; 55 and65 ms. Sum first times for common second; add each first time for third.
