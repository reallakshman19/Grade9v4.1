Vibrating sources: excitation, frequency and coupling to air

Phenomenon

A vibrating ruler can remain visibly in motion while the sound reaching a listener becomes much weaker.

Scientific construction

A sound source is a mechanical system capable of transferring energy into its surroundings. A plucked string moves back and forth, a stretched membrane oscillates, and a vibrating reed or changing airflow can excite an air column. The disturbance moves through a material medium to a receiver. The material of the source is not carried to the listener. The source can keep vibrating even when coupling to the surrounding medium becomes weak.

A periodic source repeats its state after a period T; frequency f counts full repetitions per second. A sound that is more complicated than a sinusoid may contain multiple frequency components. A string, membrane and air column can have different mode shapes and may produce similar fundamental frequencies. Hearing a frequency does not uniquely identify an instrument or the mechanism by which it was excited. A string may be plucked, struck or bowed; similar periodic motion can result from different driving methods.

Separate the causal links: energy input causes source motion, the moving boundary excites a disturbance, the medium transports it, and the receiver converts it into a measured signal. A large source displacement need not imply a large pressure signal far away. Source area, geometry, coupling, intervening medium and detector response matter. A sensor attached to the source measures a different quantity from a microphone in air. A microphone voltage is not automatically a pressure amplitude without calibration.

To observe an oscillation, label displacement and time axes, count complete repetitions between matching phase points, and convert units. A source and receiver time trace may have the same repetition frequency with a delay if a single steady tone propagates through a stationary medium and the receiver response is suitable. A pulse envelope can establish arrival time; two periodic sinusoidal traces alone can have a phase delay ambiguous by whole periods. This distinction will be developed in PH08-04 and PH08-07. A quiet receiver signal could reflect weaker source vibration, changed coupling, propagation obstruction or a sensor fault. A controlled test keeps source motion and receiver conditions matched while varying one link.

For a source in a bell jar as air is removed, an ideal vacuum cannot carry sound through the gas path, yet mechanical vibration may continue and structure-borne sound may still travel through solid supports. Do not infer that the source stopped merely because an external microphone signal weakened. Use direct motion measurement and path controls. Avoid high sound levels in demonstrations. Build the model

  1. Identify source, drive, medium and receiver. Why: They are distinct components of the causal chain.
  2. Measure source oscillation independently of receiver output. Why: A weak receiver signal alone does not establish weaker source motion.
  3. Count same-phase repetitions and identify the recorded variable. Why: Frequency is determined by repetition, not graph height.
  4. Control a single coupling or propagation link and test competing explanations. Why: Independent observations distinguish source change from path or sensor change.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A membrane completes 12 cycles in 0.06 s. Find frequency and period.

200 Hz; 0.005 s.

Independent check: 12×0.005=0.06 s.

CONNECTED

A string oscillates with unchanged measured period and displacement, but enclosing it reduces microphone output. Does this prove its frequency changed?

No. The source measurement is unchanged; enclosure may alter coupling or propagation. Check calibrated microphone and path.

Independent check: Compare source traces independently of receiver output.

ADVANCED

Two sensors record the same steady 250 Hz tone, with one trace apparently delayed 1 ms. Is travel delay unique?

Period 4 ms; delays 1 ms+4n ms can give the same periodic phase offset. A pulse or independent path length constrains n.

Independent check: Use a timed onset, not just steady phase.

Misconception and repair

A weaker sound at the listener means the source stopped vibrating; equal frequency identifies the same instrument.

Can a source vibration sensor remain unchanged while a microphone output falls?

Separate excitation, coupling, propagation and detection; use independent measurements.

Limits

  • Periodic source model only when repetition is stable; nonperiodic pulses require other descriptors.
  • Microphone voltage requires calibration before pressure comparisons.
  • Same-frequency claim assumes steady transmission and suitable detector; phase delay can be ambiguous.
  • A vacuum removes gas-borne sound but not necessarily solid-borne bypasses or electronic artifacts.

Exit check

A source completes five intervals between the first and sixth matching peaks in 20 ms. Find f.

Check reasoning

250 Hz. T=4 ms; f=1/0.004.
