Phenomenon

Two instruments can play the same note yet sound different, and a modified instrument may change pitch or loudness independently.

Scientific construction

A musical instrument is a system that excites mechanical vibration and couples it to the surrounding medium. A string instrument can be plucked or bowed; a membrane can be struck; a reed or air jet can excite an air column. The vibrating object and the air that carries sound to the listener are not the same thing. A sounding board may couple a vibrating string more effectively to air, changing radiated sound without requiring the fundamental frequency to change.

Pitch is associated mainly with repetition frequency or fundamental frequency for a simple periodic tone. Timbre describes differences in sound quality arising in part from the relative strengths of frequency components, their evolution in time and the attack/decay envelope. Two instruments may produce the same fundamental frequency but different overtones and envelopes; a listener may distinguish them even at similar pitch and loudness. A microphone spectrum is an instrument-dependent measurement: compare calibrated or consistently normalized data before attributing amplitude differences to the source. Loudness is a perceptual quantity, not directly a spectral peak height.

Changing an instrument can affect several variables at once. Shortening the vibrating length of an ideal string at fixed tension and mass per length raises its fundamental frequency. Tightening a string at fixed length and mass per length also raises its fundamental, whereas increasing mass per length lowers it. In an ideal fixed-end string, f=(1/(2L))√(F/μ). This formula is an explicitly supplied enrichment model; the qualitative instrument lesson does not require detailed standing-wave theory. For air columns, effective vibrating length and open/closed boundary conditions affect resonant frequencies; an open hole can change effective length, but real end corrections and geometry matter. Do not use the string formula for an air column.

When a string is plucked harder, its initial amplitude and radiated sound may increase, but in the small-amplitude linear approximation its normal frequencies do not change solely because of amplitude. A real string can change tension or effective stiffness at large amplitude, so the ideal claim has a limit. Compare equal source conditions and measure both the temporal period and spectrum; if an altered body changes coupling, a source-motion measurement can distinguish frequency change from radiation change. A spectrum showing peaks at integer multiples of a fundamental suggests a harmonic structure, but one measured peak alone need not identify the fundamental or the instrument.

A fair instrument comparison records source length, tension or boundary conditions, excitation, receiver position and sensor calibration. A controlled intervention and independent observation can distinguish a shifted resonant frequency from stronger excitation or changed acoustic coupling. Build the model

  1. Identify the vibrating element, excitation and coupling mechanism. Why: Instrument source and propagating medium are distinct.
  2. Read fundamental/repetition and frequency components independently of amplitude. Why: Pitch and timbre respond to different features of the vibration.
  3. Choose the qualitative length/tension model or the explicitly supplied ideal relation. Why: Changing one variable can affect frequency while another affects radiated amplitude.
  4. Compare an independent source-motion or spectral observation. Why: Matched conditions are necessary to distinguish excitation, resonance and coupling.

Misconception and repair

Higher amplitude means higher pitch, and equal pitch means identical instrument.

Can two waveforms have equal repetition period but different spectral composition?

Measure period and spectral components separately, and control the vibrating geometry and coupling.

Limits

  • Small-amplitude, ideal fixed-end uniform string only for supplied quantitative relation.
  • Real instrument boundary corrections, damping, stiffness and coupling can alter ideal predictions.
  • Spectral measurements depend on microphone response and position; pitch/loudness/timbre not interchangeable.
  • Do not infer a unique instrument from one frequency or one spectrum alone.
