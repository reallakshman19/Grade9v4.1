Phenomenon

A moving source can change the frequency received by a stationary observer, while a stationary resonator selects particular vibration modes.

Scientific construction

EXTENSION — quantitative Doppler effect, standing waves and resonance are higher-level comparison material, not established compulsory NSEJS content. The Doppler effect is a change in observed frequency when source and observer motion alter the rate of arriving wavefronts. Work in the rest frame of a stationary medium. A source emitting at frequency f_s emits successive wavefronts separated in time by T_s=1/f_s. During that period a previous wavefront advances vT_s; if the source moves toward a stationary observer by u_sT_s, the next wavefront starts closer to the previous one. Ahead of a subsonic source, their spacing is (v−u_s)T_s. A stationary observer sees wavefronts pass at speed v, so f_obs=vf_s/(v−u_s). Behind the source the spacing is (v+u_s)T_s and the observed frequency is lower. These expressions assume uniform straight motion and a stationary medium, with source speed below wave speed.

A moving observer with stationary source is a different model. The emitted spacing remains v/f_s in the medium; an observer moving toward the wavefronts meets them at a greater relative speed, giving f_obs=(v+u_o)f_s/v for that specified geometry. Do not treat source and observer speeds as interchangeable in all acoustic cases. If motion directions or medium flow differ, derive the arrival rate using a consistent frame. Amplitude or intensity changes alone do not establish Doppler frequency shifts.

Standing waves arise from superposition of oppositely travelling waves of the same frequency under suitable boundary conditions. In an ideal fixed-end string, displacement nodes are at both ends. Allowed wavelengths satisfy L=nλ/2, giving f_n=nv/(2L). In an ideal open-open air column, displacement antinodes occur near both open ends, with the same frequency sequence in the simple model. In an ideal closed-open air column, displacement node at the closed end and antinode at the open end give L=(2n−1)λ/4, so only odd multiples of the lowest frequency occur. Pressure boundary descriptions are complementary; do not mix pressure nodes with displacement nodes. Real air columns have end corrections and losses.

Resonance occurs when periodic forcing excites a mode efficiently near its natural frequency. Damping affects the strength and width of the response; the driving frequency determines steady forced oscillation frequency in a linear model. A large amplitude need not prove a particular mode unless the mode shape, boundary conditions and excitation are known. If only some spectral peaks are visible, missing modes may be weakly excited or below threshold rather than forbidden. A mode identification problem must compare complete candidate frequency sets and additional mode-shape evidence.

Advanced reasoning connects frequency shifts with mode selection without conflating the two mechanisms. A moving source can deliver a shifted forcing frequency to a stationary resonator. Determine wavefront spacing first, then compare the received frequency with the possible modes of the resonator. A stationary instrument with a changed length can produce a different natural frequency without any Doppler shift. Independent source motion or boundary evidence distinguishes these models. Build the model

  1. Draw successive emissions and motion relative to the medium. Why: Source motion changes front spacing; observer motion changes encounter rate.
  2. Compute received frequency using the appropriate wavefront model. Why: Do not apply moving-source and moving-observer expressions interchangeably.
  3. Draw displacement nodes/antinodes and derive allowed wavelength family. Why: Boundary type determines half-wave versus odd-quarter-wave condition.
  4. Compare received forcing with mode predictions and check independent evidence. Why: A frequency match is conditional evidence of resonance, not unique identification of all properties.

Misconception and repair

Source and observer motion produce identical formulas, every tube allows every harmonic, and every large signal proves resonance.

Does a moving observer change the spacing emitted into stationary air? Which end of a closed-open tube is a displacement node?

Derive wavefront spacing or encounter rate separately; impose the actual boundary conditions and compare frequency/mode evidence.
