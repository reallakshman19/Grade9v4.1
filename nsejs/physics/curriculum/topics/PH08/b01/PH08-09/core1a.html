Echo and reverberation: arrival overlap and detectability

Phenomenon

A short clap may produce a distinct repeat in one room but a long blended sound in another.

Scientific construction

When a source produces a short pulse in a room, part of the sound arrives directly and parts reflect from surfaces. A sufficiently delayed detectable return may be heard as a distinct echo. Numerous overlapping reflected arrivals can prolong the sound after the source stops; this persistence is reverberation. The distinction depends on the source signal, the listener or detector, the strength of returns and their timing. The familiar school estimate of a tenth of a second for a separately perceived echo is a rough contextual guideline, not an exact universal rule.

Draw a time axis beginning at emission. A direct arrival has a travel time L_direct/v; a reflected arrival has L_ref/v. The difference is the excess delay, not necessarily the full return time. A pulse of duration τ occupies an interval from its leading arrival t to t+τ if propagation does not substantially change its shape. Two finite pulses overlap if their intervals intersect; even nonoverlapping intervals may not be distinguishable if the detector needs a minimum quiet gap or a return is too weak. For a specified model with required quiet gap g, successive nonoverlapping pulses are separately resolved only when their leading times differ by at least τ+g. This is a stipulated detector rule, not a claim about all human hearing.

An example: pulses of duration 12 ms start at 0, 8 and 40 ms relative to the direct arrival. The first two overlap from 8 to12 ms and together occupy 0–20 ms; the third begins 20 ms after that cluster ends. If all are detectable and the specified quiet-gap criterion is 5 ms, the third is separable from the earlier cluster. A pulse starting only 3 ms after the cluster ends would not be separately resolved by that rule even though it does not overlap. In analysing a series of reflections, update the cluster end to the latest end of any pulse that overlaps or lies within the specified quiet-gap threshold.

Absorbent wall treatments can reduce reflected amplitudes and late energy, usually reducing reverberation. They do not necessarily change path travel times significantly when geometry and medium stay fixed. A weak reflection can be physically present but absent from the detector's record. Moving the source, changing a wall, or changing pulse duration can change overlap and perceived clarity without necessarily altering every path. Avoid using loud sounds in demonstrations; reason from recorded or hypothetical data.

For advanced tasks, compare two proposed room or detector models by predicting arrival intervals and thresholded observations, then use an independent change of duration or sensitivity to eliminate alternatives. Distinguish an observed unresolved cluster from a claim that there is only one path. Build the model

  1. Identify direct and reflected leading times, pulse duration and detection threshold. Why: Arrival geometry and instrument response are separate inputs.
  2. Construct pulse intervals and group overlap or insufficient quiet gaps. Why: A cluster can contain several physically distinct paths.
  3. Apply the stated amplitude and resolution rules to predicted records. Why: Detectability depends on both signal strength and temporal separation.
  4. Test a changed source duration or detector threshold against rival path models. Why: An independent observation can distinguish previously unresolved returns.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

Direct and reflected pulses start 15 ms apart; pulse duration 20 ms. Do intervals overlap?

Yes, by 5 ms.

Independent check: First interval 0–20 ms; second 15–35 ms.

CONNECTED

Pulses start at 0, 10, 35 ms, duration 8 ms, quiet gap 5 ms required. Group the record.

First two separated by only 2 ms quiet gap: one unresolved group; third starts 17 ms after second ends, separate.

Independent check: Group 0–18 ms and 35–43 ms.

ADVANCED

Two rooms have identical visible leading times 0 and 40 ms, but one may have a weak return at 20 ms below threshold. What test distinguishes them?

Lower a calibrated detection threshold with sufficiently short pulse and quiet-gap rule; an additional detected 20 ms return supports the weak-path model.

Independent check: A missing return at original threshold is not evidence of absence.

Misconception and repair

Each separate reflected path is automatically a distinct audible echo; a missing peak proves no reflected path exists.

Could two 20 ms pulses starting 10 ms apart be resolved as separate echoes? Could a weak path fall below threshold?

Build arrival intervals and apply explicit resolution and amplitude conditions before interpreting the record.

Limits

  • Linear pulse-duration model assumes negligible distortion and known leading times; real signals can interfere or change shape.
  • Quiet-gap and amplitude thresholds are explicitly specified task models, not universal human criteria.
  • Use relative arrival times consistently; absolute time from emission differs from excess delay.
  • Absorption can reduce detectable returns without removing physical paths; amplitude alone does not determine distance.

Exit check

Three detectable pulses start 0, 7 and 30 ms, each 10 ms long; required quiet gap is 4 ms. How many resolved groups?

Check reasoning

Two: 0–17 ms and 30–40 ms. First two overlap; third has 13 ms gap.
