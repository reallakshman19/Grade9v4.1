Phenomenon

A travelling periodic profile may look unchanged at a later time although energy propagates.

Scientific construction

A snapshot of a travelling longitudinal sound wave can display each particle's signed displacement from equilibrium against its equilibrium position x. The vertical coordinate is a graph value, not the physical vertical position of the particle. A pressure snapshot instead records excess pressure. They are not the same physical quantity and their extrema need not coincide. Both may be periodic in the simple sinusoidal model; select the variable before interpreting any graph. The amplitude of a displacement wave is its maximum absolute displacement from equilibrium. Peak-to-peak distance on the vertical scale is twice that amplitude. Horizontal spacing of matching phase points determines wavelength; a crest and neighbouring trough are half a wavelength apart.

A useful way to reason is to imagine a transparent tracing of the snapshot. If a pattern moves right at constant speed v, after time t the tracing has shifted right by vt; if the signed velocity u is negative, shift left. The field value at a fixed coordinate x is the value originally at x−ut. Thus D(x,t)=D(x−ut,0). This is a statement about propagation of a pattern; one parcel of air does not travel at the wave speed. For example, a wave pattern can move metres while the local displacement amplitude is millimetres. In a sinusoidal profile, a crest initially at x0 has equivalent crests at x0+nλ, where n is any integer; after t their coordinates are x0+ut+nλ. This construction separates the travelling pattern from local oscillation.

An integer-wavelength translation reproduces the same graph at every coordinate. That does not mean the wave stopped. A half-wavelength shift changes a sinusoid's sign; a quarter-wavelength shift moves zero crossings to extrema. If a right-moving wave advances 1.5 wavelengths, the observable snapshot shift can be represented by half a wavelength, although the actual propagation distance was larger. A finite isolated pulse does not have this periodic ambiguity: its unique labelled feature can be followed directly.

The inverse problem is harder. Two snapshots showing equivalent phase markers at x1 and x2 give possible signed shifts x2−x1+nλ, not necessarily the smallest visible separation. For a time interval Δt, possible velocities are (x2−x1+nλ)/Δt. A declared speed range excludes candidates that violate it. An independent third snapshot at a different time tests the remaining candidates: compute where the same phase set would be and compare with the observation modulo λ. If more than one remains, report the ambiguity instead of guessing. A fixed detector's known period or an independently measured direction may also constrain the solution. Measurement uncertainty broadens the possible ranges and may prevent unique identification. These inverse constructions extend, rather than copy, a currently available Core2 wave-snapshot candidate.

In a laboratory, label axes and units, identify same-phase features, record propagation direction if known, and distinguish measured intervals from assumed numbers of cycles. A complete solution checks the sign of translation, units of distance and time, and the relevant periodic boundary. An independent check uses another phase marker or time observation, not repetition of the same arithmetic.

Build the model

  1. Identify field and same-phase spatial interval. Why: The graph axes distinguish longitudinal displacement from physical geometry; repeated phase establishes λ.
  2. Translate all phase families by the signed pattern shift uΔt. Why: A travelling field is D(x,t)=D(x−ut,0), not a particle trajectory.
  3. For an inverse problem enumerate shifts differing by nλ and apply the speed range. Why: Periodic repetition makes all those shifts observationally equivalent in two snapshots.
  4. Test remaining candidates against an independent observation and report nonuniqueness if necessary. Why: An additional time or phase constraint may reject aliases; it does not guarantee uniqueness.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A displacement snapshot has a crest at x=0.12 m, the adjacent trough at x=0.32 m, and maximum displacement 3 mm. Give λ and peak-to-peak displacement.

λ=2(0.32−0.12)=0.40 m. Peak-to-peak displacement=6 mm.

Independent check: The next crest is at 0.52 m; the two crest positions differ by 0.40 m.

CONNECTED

A sinusoidal pattern with λ=0.50 m moves left at 300 m/s. A crest starts at x=0.10 m. After 2.5 ms, list crest coordinates in 0≤x<1 m.

Shift is −0.75 m, so the crest family is −0.65+0.50n m; visible crests are 0.35 and 0.85 m.

Independent check: The shift modulo λ is −0.25 m (equivalent to +0.25); original crests at 0.10 and 0.60 become 0.35 and 0.85.

ADVANCED

A periodic snapshot has λ=0.50 m. After 4 ms the crest set appears displaced +0.10 m modulo λ. A speed instrument bounds |u| between 80 and 300 m/s. After 1 ms the crest offset is +0.40 m modulo λ. Find u.

Candidates from first pair: (0.10+0.50n)/0.004; n=1 gives 150, n=2 gives 275, n=−1 gives −100, n=−2 gives −225 m/s; n=0 gives 25 outside range. At 1 ms the respective offsets modulo 0.50 are 0.15, 0.275, 0.40, 0.275. Only −100 matches.

Independent check: At 4 ms −100×0.004=−0.40 m, equivalent to +0.10; at 1 ms −0.10 m is equivalent to +0.40.

Misconception and repair

The same snapshot implies a stationary wave pattern; the nearest crest identifies the same physical particle.

Can a periodic profile reproduce itself while a disturbance continues to propagate? What would a marked particle do?

Use a signed translation of the field and a family of equivalent phase positions. A marked particle oscillates locally; additional observations may be needed to determine the pattern shift.

Limits

  • Uniform stationary medium, one stable travelling sinusoidal displacement wave; signed pattern velocity is constant in each task.
  • The vertical graph coordinate is longitudinal displacement along the propagation axis, not a transverse particle path; pressure and displacement profiles are different fields.
  • A periodic snapshot identifies shifts only modulo the wavelength; a unique speed requires additional information.
  • A stationary sensor's time trace and a spatial snapshot are not interchangeable; no bulk particle transport is inferred.
  • Phase shift convention: positive signed shift is toward increasing x. Only a uniform repeating pattern returns after whole wavelengths.

Exit check

A wave with λ=0.60 m has identical profiles 3 ms apart. If its rightward speed is between 150 and 500 m/s, what speeds remain possible?

Check reasoning

200 and 400 m/s. u(0.003)=n(0.60), so u=200n m/s, with n=1 or 2 under the given bounds.
