Phenomenon

An echo delay measures a reflected propagation path; the distance to a reflector depends on the source and receiver geometry.

Scientific construction

Sound reflects when a wave meets a suitable boundary. A short pulse can arrive directly at a receiver and later along a reflected path. The echo is not a new sound emitted by the reflector; it is a returned part of the disturbance. A large smooth wall can be modelled as a specular reflector when its dimensions and surface conditions are appropriate. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection for the ideal ray model; roughness, diffraction and absorption can weaken or spread the pulse.

For a stationary source and receiver at the same location facing a planar wall, a normal reflected path goes a distance d to the wall and d back. The elapsed time since emission is 2d/v, so d=vt/2. This is not a universal echo formula. A receiver in another location sees a total reflected path equal to source-to-reflection-point plus reflection-point-to-receiver. If the recorded time is measured from the direct arrival rather than emission, add the direct travel time before inferring reflected length. In particular, the difference between two arrival times is a path-length difference, not the whole reflected path.

A plane reflection can be analysed using a virtual image of the source behind the wall. Reflect the source across the wall; a straight segment from this image to the real receiver has length equal to the two-leg reflected path. Where the segment crosses the wall determines the reflection point, provided the wall is sufficiently large. For source and receiver the same perpendicular distance d from the wall and separation b parallel to it, the reflected path is sqrt((2d)^2+b^2). The direct path is b. If excess delay Δt is measured, reflected length is b+vΔt. Solve for d only if the given geometry supports it. This geometric extension is taught as authored advanced reasoning.

If source and receiver are at different normal distances d_s and d_m on the same side, with separation b parallel to the wall, the image construction gives reflected length sqrt((d_s+d_m)^2+b^2) and direct length sqrt((d_s−d_m)^2+b^2). Equal measured lengths or delays can fit more than one arrangement when position information is missing. Draw the source, receiver, wall and timing reference before choosing an equation; then check the path actually reaches the reflector and the inferred geometry is physically valid.

An observer can distinguish two arrivals when the pulse duration, detector resolution and separation are appropriate. Human echo perception is not a universal exact timing threshold. The simple relations assume stationary objects and uniform propagation speed. Multiple reflection and moving geometries require additional path modelling, addressed in PH08-12. Build the model

  1. Draw source, receiver, wall and timing origin. Why: Travel time corresponds to path length from emission, not a label on a diagram.
  2. Reflect the source in the wall and locate the virtual straight path. Why: Specular geometry makes the virtual path equal in length to the two real legs.
  3. Convert measured delay into total path or path difference as appropriate. Why: A direct-arrival reference subtracts the direct transit time.
  4. Solve the geometric constraint and check admissible distances and observation limits. Why: Nonnegative lengths and adequate wall extent are required; missing geometry may make the inference nonunique.

Worked examples

FOUNDATION

A colocated pulse source and sensor receive a normal echo 0.080 s after emission. v=340 m/s. Find wall distance.

Reflected length 27.2 m; wall distance 13.6 m.

Independent check: 13.6 m each way takes 0.080 s in total.

CONNECTED

Source and receiver are 6 m apart parallel to a wall, both 4 m away. v=340 m/s. Find direct and reflected lengths and excess delay.

Direct 6 m; reflected sqrt(8²+6²)=10 m; excess delay 4/340≈11.76 ms.

Independent check: Reflected time≈29.41 ms and direct≈17.65 ms.

ADVANCED

Source and receiver both lie distance d from a wall and are 8 m apart parallel to it. v=320 m/s, excess delay 12.5 ms. Find d.

Reflected length 8+4=12 m. 4d²=144−64=80, d=sqrt(20)≈4.47 m.

Independent check: sqrt((2d)²+8²)=12 m; difference from direct 4 m.

Misconception and repair

Every echo delay gives distance vt/2, regardless of receiver position or timing reference.

A receiver is separated from the source; the direct pulse arrives before the reflected pulse. Does the time between them measure the full reflected path?

Construct virtual reflection geometry and add direct time if needed before solving the physical distances.

Limits

  • Uniform stationary medium and stationary source, receiver and reflector; no multiple reflections unless stated.
  • Specular ray model for sufficiently large planar wall; wall intersection must lie on the reflecting surface.
  • Delay measured after direct arrival is not elapsed time since emission.
  • Finite pulse duration, attenuation and detector resolution may prevent separation of returns.

Exit check

Source and receiver 3 m apart parallel to a wall are each 2 m from it. v=340 m/s. Find reflected length and excess delay.

Check reasoning

5 m and 2/340≈5.88 ms. Virtual perpendicular separation 4 m, parallel separation 3 m; direct path 3 m.
