Surface gravity and planetary scaling

A1 · Observe and ask

A person weighing 600 N on Earth would weigh only about 100 N on the Moon (about 1/6 g), yet would weigh approximately 1500 N on Jupiter (about 2.5 g). Even though Jupiter is over 300 times more massive than Earth, its surface gravity is only 2.5 times greater because its radius is also roughly 11 times larger.

Learning target

Derive g = G * M / R^2 = (4/3) * pi * G * rho * R and evaluate ratio shifts when mass, radius, and density change simultaneously.

A3 · Construct the model

Surface gravitational acceleration g is determined by equating the gravitational force on a test mass m to its weight m*g: m*g = G * M * m / R^2 ==> g = G * M / R^2. Expressing planet mass in terms of uniform density rho and volume V = (4/3)*pi*R^3 gives g = (4/3) * pi * G * rho * R. Thus g scales proportionally to M/R^2, or equivalently to rho * R.

A4 · Derive and check the rule

Surface acceleration: g = G * M / R^2 = (4/3) * pi * G * rho * R. Dimensions: [g] = L T^-2 (m s^-2). Applicability: non-rotating, spherically symmetric celestial bodies with negligible atmospheric buoyancy.

Check the frame, sign convention, unit and applicability before accepting a numerical result. For a new model the original scientific warrant still requires independent review.

A5 · Read the physical representation

A6 · Three progressive worked examples

FOUNDATION

Question: A planet has the same radius as Earth but twice the mass. Find its surface gravity in terms of Earth's surface gravity g0.

Reasoned solution: g = G * (2M) / R^2 = 2 * (G * M / R^2) = 2 * g0.

CONNECTED

Question: Planet X has mass 2M and radius 2R. What is the ratio g_Earth / g_X?

Reasoned solution: g_X = G * (2M) / (2R)^2 = (2/4) * (G * M / R^2) = 0.5 * g_Earth ==> g_Earth / g_X = 2 : 1.

ADVANCED

Question: Planet Y has mean density twice that of Earth, and its radius is half that of Earth. Find its surface gravity.

Reasoned solution: Using g = (4/3)*pi*G*rho*R: g_Y / g_Earth = (rho_Y / rho_Earth) * (R_Y / R_Earth) = 2 * (1/2) = 1. Its surface gravity is exactly equal to Earth's.

A7 · Worked anchor and independent check

Problem: The mass and radius of Planet 1 are M and R respectively. The mass and radius of Planet 2 are 3M and 2R respectively. A pendulum clock that ticks once per second on Planet 1 is taken to Planet 2. Does the clock run faster or slower, and what is its new period?

Solution and check: Surface gravity on Planet 2: g2 = G * (3M) / (2R)^2 = (3/4) * g1 = 0.75 g1. Pendulum period T = 2*pi*sqrt(L/g), so T2/T1 = sqrt(g1/g2) = sqrt(4/3) ≈ 1.155 s. The period is longer, so the clock ticks slower.

A8 · Diagnose a tempting wrong idea

Tempting claim: If a planet has 4 times the volume of Earth with the same density, its surface gravity will be 4 times greater.

Why it fails: Volume scaling as R^3 means 4 times volume implies radius increases by 4^(1/3) ≈ 1.587. Since g scales with rho * R, surface gravity increases only by factor 4^(1/3) ≈ 1.587, not factor 4.

Repair: Distinguish between mass scaling and radius scaling: g = (4/3)*pi*G*rho*R depends linearly on radius when density is constant.

A9 · Counterexample, limit and synthesis

Changed condition

Can a planet with smaller mass have a higher surface gravity than Earth?

Yes. If its radius is sufficiently small. For example, a white dwarf with 0.5 Earth masses but 0.01 Earth radius has g = 0.5 / (0.01^2) * g0 = 5000 g0.

Independent exit check

If Earth's radius shrinks by 1% while its total mass remains constant, by what percentage does surface gravity change?

Reveal the reasoned exit answer

Since g = G*M / R^2 = G*M*R^-2, small fractional change is dg/g ≈ -2 * (dR/R). For dR/R = -1%, dg/g ≈ -2 * (-1%) = +2%. Surface gravity increases by approximately 2%.
