1. A journey can have two different answers

M01 learning unit: Core1A textbook · Core1B protected practice (3 INSTANCE tasks)

Suppose you walk 80 metres east to a library, then turn back 30 metres west to a bus stop. Your shoes have carried you 110 metres along the ground. Yet the bus stop is only 50 metres east of where you began.

Both measurements are true. They answer different questions. Distance describes the total length of your path; displacement describes the change from starting position to finishing position. One tracks the journey; the other tracks the overall change in position.

Scientist's habit: Before writing an equation, define an object, an observer and a positive direction. A number such as “+50 m” becomes meaningful only after that choice.

Coordinate calibration: origin, scale and orientation

Extension for problem solving: the fixed origin, metre unit and reference direction are foundational. The two-anchor calibration and consistency test extend those ideas; they are not asserted to be a mandatory syllabus item.

A coordinate is a label for position relative to a chosen origin and direction. To compare readings from different rulers, first identify shared physical markers. A uniform ruler has a constant signed scale: subtract two paired readings to cancel the offset and determine the scale. One anchor then determines the offset. This explains why a shift of origin does not change displacement and why reversing an axis reverses its signed component. The physical separation remains the absolute difference in calibrated metre coordinates. Calibration is a model: two marks alone do not establish that every intermediate mark on a damaged ruler is equally spaced. A third independent check can invalidate the assumption. This is distinct from measuring the length of the travelled path.

x = b + kr; k = (x₂ − x₁)/(r₂ − r₁)

Worked examples: from foundation to advanced

Foundation: A gate is x=0; a flag 4 m west is marked with a west-positive ruler of unit spacing. Compare readings. Ground east-positive x=−4 m; west-positive r=+4 m. Check: Both give a 4 m separation from the gate.

Connected: A metre ruler has zero at x=7 m and increases eastward. Convert readings −2 and +3. x=7+r; positions 5 m and 10 m, displacement +5 m. Check: Subtracting cancels the offset.

Advanced: A uniform scale reads 1 at x=3 m and 5 at x=15 m. Find x at reading 4 and check a third marker reading 7 at x=21 m. k=3 m/division, b=0; reading 4 gives 12 m; reading 7 gives 21 m. Check: The third marker is consistent but does not prove uniformity everywhere.

Common error: All rulers reporting different signed positions must disagree physically. Translate both into one physical axis before comparing; distinguish sign from length.
Check your understanding: A uniform scale reads 0 at x=9 m and 3 at x=3 m. Find x at reading 5.

x=−1 m. k=−2 m/division and b=9 m; x=9−2(5).

2. Position, path and displacement

M02 learning unit: Core1A textbook · Core1B protected practice (3 INSTANCE tasks)

Place a number line along a straight road, with the school's gate at zero and east positive. A bus at the 120 m marker has position x = +120 m. If it ends at x = −20 m, its displacement is −140 m, even if it takes a longer, indirect route.

Displacement Δx = final position − initial position

Displacement is a signed quantity in one dimension. The negative sign reports direction; it is not a declaration of an error. Distance is never negative because it adds up lengths of actual travel.

A route with several turns is handled one segment at a time. For example, +8 m, −13 m and +9 m produce net displacement +4 m, but a distance of 30 m. If a traveller returns to the starting point, displacement is zero while distance can be very large.

Why does this distinction matter at NSEJS level?

Many multi-step questions involve a vehicle going forward, reversing or waiting. Using total distance where displacement is required makes even a well-executed calculation physically wrong. The decisive skill is not recognizing a formula: it is identifying the quantity that the situation actually asks for.

What a position log cannot tell you

A signed position change is determined by the initial and final points alone. Distance depends on the route: every piece of actual motion contributes a nonnegative length. Break a straight route at each real reversal, not just at each convenient recording time. Adding signed changes cancels intermediate positions, whereas adding their magnitudes preserves travel. The triangle inequality establishes the endpoint lower bound. If the logger records only some checkpoints, the sum of successive absolute changes is itself a lower bound; an unobserved turn can add distance without changing any sampled position. If motion is monotone between checkpoints, the bound is attained. This distinction matters when interpreting logs, inferring unknown turning positions, or testing claims about an incomplete journey.

D ≥ Σ |xᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ| ≥ |x_f − x_i|

Worked examples: from foundation to advanced

Foundation: A walker goes from x=−3 m to x=5 m without reversing. Δx=+8 m, distance=8 m. Check: A monotone straight trip attains the bound.

Connected: A robot records x=2,9,4 m with monotone legs. Δx=+2 m; D=7+5=12 m. Check: D≥|Δx| and the turn is included.

Advanced: Only x=0 and x=6 m are recorded. Construct routes of 6 m and 14 m. Direct 0→6 gives 6 m. Route 0→10→6 gives 10+4=14 m. Both have Δx=+6 m. Check: The observations alone do not fix total path.

Common error: Distance equals the sum of recorded position changes even when the logger misses turns. Use the sum of absolute sampled changes as a lower bound unless monotone legs are guaranteed.
Check your understanding: A robot samples x=3,−2,7 m. Find displacement and the minimum compatible distance.

Δx=+4 m; minimum 14 m. Signed sum −5+9=4; magnitude sum 5+9=14, attained by monotone legs.

3. Speed and velocity are not interchangeable

Speed tells you how quickly distance is accumulated. Velocity tells you how quickly position changes in a specified direction. To report a velocity accurately, we must say who observes the motion and what direction counts as positive.

Average speed = total distance ÷ elapsed time
Average velocity = displacement ÷ elapsed time
Worked anchor · Different averages

For the 80 m east, 30 m west walk, suppose the whole journey takes 20 seconds. The average speed is 110/20 = 5.5 m/s. The average velocity, taking east positive, is 50/20 = +2.5 m/s.

If the walker returned all the way home in 20 seconds, their average velocity would be zero, but their average speed would still be positive. Their instantaneous speed at any moment need not equal either average.

When motion is uniform along a straight line, position increases or decreases by equal amounts in equal times. If the motion turns around, the instantaneous velocity can change sign while speed remains a nonnegative magnitude.

4. Acceleration measures a change in velocity

A car can be moving east while accelerating west: it is slowing down. A car can also be moving west while accelerating west: it is speeding up. Acceleration is a signed rate of velocity change, not simply “getting faster.”

Average acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time interval

For example, a car's velocity changes uniformly from +4 m/s to −2 m/s in 3 s. Its acceleration is (−2−4)/3 = −2 m/s². During the interval it slows to rest, then reverses direction. The negative acceleration describes the direction of the change, not the sign of the speed.

Misconception to repair: “Negative acceleration always means an object is slowing down.”

Counterexample: A vehicle moving at −2 m/s increases its westward speed to −6 m/s in two seconds. Its acceleration is −2 m/s², yet its speed increases from 2 m/s to 6 m/s.

Acceleration can also arise from changing direction while speed stays constant. We will return to this with circular motion.

5. Motion graphs are physical stories

A position–time graph records where something is at each moment. Its slope indicates velocity: a steep upward segment represents a large positive velocity, a horizontal segment means the object is stationary, and a downward segment means a negative velocity.

A velocity–time graph tells a different story. Its slope represents acceleration, while the signed area between the graph and the time axis gives displacement. Segments below the axis contribute negative area. For distance, add the magnitudes of those areas instead.

Worked anchor · Translating the graph

For 4 seconds an object moves with velocity +3 m/s, then for 3 seconds it moves at −1 m/s. The first segment contributes +12 m; the second contributes −3 m. Therefore, displacement is +9 m while distance is 15 m.

Independent check: Since the object moves in opposite directions, the total distance must be greater than or equal to the magnitude of displacement. Here 15 ≥ 9, as expected.

Not every line on a graph is a physical path through space. The graph axes are mathematical quantities: a line that slopes downward on a velocity–time graph does not mean the vehicle is moving down a hill. It means velocity is decreasing in the chosen signed coordinate.

6. Derive the constant-acceleration relations

For constant acceleration, velocity changes at the same rate throughout the interval. A velocity–time graph is therefore a straight line. Its slope is a, so after time t, the final velocity is the initial velocity plus the accumulated change.

v = u + at

The displacement equals the area under that straight-line velocity graph. The area is a rectangle of height u plus a signed triangle of height at and base t. This is why the displacement formula has a one-half term rather than merely multiplying final speed by time.

Δx = ut + ½at²

Alternatively, for constant acceleration, average velocity is (u + v)/2. Multiplying by elapsed time gives Δx = ½(u + v)t. Eliminating time from the first relation then gives:

v² = u² + 2aΔx
Model boundary: These relations require constant acceleration along the chosen axis. If acceleration changes during the journey, divide the timeline into valid intervals, use the actual graph, or apply an appropriate more general model. Never treat a stage-averaged acceleration as a guarantee that the entire trajectory follows one constant-acceleration law.

A dimensional check can catch some errors: in Δx = ut + ½at², both terms have dimensions of metres. But dimensional consistency alone cannot prove that the model assumptions were true.

7. Relative motion depends on the observer

You may be stationary relative to your seat in a moving bus, while another observer on the pavement sees you moving. Neither observation is contradictory. Position and velocity are measured relative to an explicitly chosen frame.

Relative velocity of B as seen from A: v(B/A) = vB − vA

This subtraction uses signed velocities. If A moves east at +6 m/s and B moves west at −2 m/s, A sees B move at −8 m/s. If they both move east at +6 and +2 m/s, the relative velocity is −4 m/s.

Exploration · Move the observer

A cyclist B travels east at +3 m/s. Change observer A's velocity; notice that the same cyclist has different relative velocities without changing their own motion.

Use the left/right arrow keys on the slider to change its value.

Observer A+1 m/s
Cyclist B+3 m/s

From A's moving viewpoint B moves east at 2 m/s.

Why this becomes difficult: In a meeting or overtaking problem, we need the initial separation as well as the relative velocity. A correct relative-speed subtraction does not by itself tell us which motion interval contains the encounter.

8. Multi-stage motion: test every interval

Consider this new worked problem. Cyclist A starts at x = 0 and moves east at +4 m/s for 4 seconds, then stops at x = 16 m. Cyclist B starts at x = 40 m and rides west at −3 m/s without stopping. When do they meet?

Advanced worked construction · Do not skip the model-validity check

Step 1 — Make the interval model. For 0 ≤ t ≤ 4, A has position xA = 4t and B has xB = 40 − 3t. Equating these gives t = 40/7 ≈ 5.71 s.

Step 2 — Reject the impossible candidate. That answer is later than the interval in which A is moving. The equation xA = 4t is no longer true after 4 seconds; therefore 5.71 seconds is not a solution to the actual journey.

Step 3 — Construct the new stage. After t = 4 s, A stays at x = 16 m. B continues along xB = 40 − 3t. Set 16 = 40 − 3t. Hence they meet at t = 8 seconds, at x = 16 m.

Step 4 — Check both positions. At t = 8, A remains at x = 16 and B has position 40 − 24 = 16 m. Both objects occupy the same place at the same time, and the model is valid for t ≥ 4.

The important thinking move was not algebraic difficulty. It was recognizing that a mathematically correct solution to an equation can be physically invalid when used outside its assumed time interval.

Misconception to repair: “If two position equations give the same x at a calculated time, the bodies meet.”

The equations must represent the bodies' actual motion at that time. An equation for the first 4 seconds cannot be extrapolated unchanged to 8 seconds if the body has stopped.

9. Uniform circular motion is not constant velocity

Walk around a circular track at constant speed. Your speed may be unchanged, yet your direction changes continuously. Velocity includes direction, so velocity is changing even when the speedometer reading is steady.

For a circle of radius r and one revolution completed in time T, the distance travelled in one revolution is 2πr. Average speed over that revolution is 2πr/T, while the displacement over a complete revolution is zero, so average velocity is zero.

This distinction makes a powerful bridge from kinematics to Newton's laws. A changing velocity requires acceleration; the physical interaction causing that directional change is explored in PH02. Quantitative centripetal-force extensions are tracked separately until their syllabus and QRT scope are reviewed.

10. Investigations: think before revealing

These are textbook self-checks, not protected Core1B attempts and not authenticated NSEJS questions. Try each first; then open the worked explanation. Dedicated QRT tasks use separate protected attempt storage.

Investigation A · Can the speed be large while the average velocity is zero?

A runner completes one full lap of a circular track in 60 seconds. Explain whether their average speed and average velocity can both be zero.

Reveal the reasoning and check

The runner covers the nonzero circumference of the track, so average speed is positive. The final position equals the starting position, so displacement and therefore average velocity are zero. The answer depends on which quantity is being averaged.

Investigation B · Change the positive direction

A vehicle has velocity −5 m/s and acceleration −1 m/s². Predict whether its speed grows during the next second, assuming constant acceleration.

Reveal the reasoning and check

After 1 s, velocity is −6 m/s. Its speed increases from 5 m/s to 6 m/s. The negative acceleration points in the same direction as the negative velocity, so the magnitude grows. Reversing the coordinate axis would change both signs, not the physical conclusion.

Investigation C · A graph with negative area

Velocity is +2 m/s for 5 s and −3 m/s for 2 s. Find signed displacement, total distance, and average velocity over the seven-second interval.

Reveal the reasoning and check

First displacement: +10 m. Second displacement: −6 m. Net displacement: +4 m. Distance: 10 + 6 = 16 m. Average velocity: 4/7 m/s east. Since motion reverses, the distance is greater than |displacement|.

Investigation D · When does the encounter occur?

A starts at 0 and moves east at 5 m/s for 3 s, then waits at x = 15 m. B begins at x = 36 m and moves west at 2 m/s. Find their meeting time and explain why an apparently simpler calculation fails.

Reveal the reasoning and check

If both continued moving, 5t = 36−2t gives 36/7 ≈ 5.14 s, which falls outside A's 0–3 s moving stage. After 3 s, A is fixed at x=15. Solving 15 = 36−2t gives 10.5 s. Substituting into both actual position models confirms the encounter.

Investigation E · Prove or refute

Someone argues: “If the average velocity is positive, the vehicle must have moved only in the positive direction.” Is that true under every circumstance?

Reveal the reasoning and check

No. Positive average velocity only requires net displacement to be positive. A vehicle may move +20 m, then −8 m, for net displacement +12 m. A single example refutes the universal claim, because the claim said “must” or “every.”
