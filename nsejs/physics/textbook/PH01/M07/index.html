1. Observe and establish a reference

Two moving observers can assign different velocities to the same object, while a mathematical encounter time may fail because the motion model changes.

Prerequisite bridge: PH01-M01; PH01-M02; PH01-M03; PH01-M05. Use a fixed reference axis and consistent SI units unless stated otherwise.

Learning objective: Compose velocities in a shared frame, translate position/velocity histories between uniformly moving frames, and validate piecewise meeting events.

2. Build and justify the scientific model

The ground and a uniformly moving observer can assign different positions and velocities to the same object. In a common one-dimensional axis, x_rel=x_object−x_observer and v_rel=v_object−v_observer. These differences follow by subtracting the observer position at the same time; a constant observer velocity shifts every v–t segment by the same amount. A simultaneous separation between two objects and their meeting event are invariant under this shared translation, but an individual object's displacement and path distance need not be. For piecewise motion, build each position law from its actual boundary position and use one global clock. Solve for equality only on the interval where both laws apply. If a candidate root falls outside that interval, reject it and rebuild using the correct stage.

On a shared axis x_rel=x−x_O and v_rel=v−v_O. For stage start at t_b with x_b, constant stage velocity v_b gives x=x_b+v_b(t−t_b). A meeting is equality of positions at one common time within the model domains.

Derivation and reasoning

Subtract simultaneous coordinates: x_rel=x−x_O. Subtract signed velocities measured in that common frame: v_rel=v−v_O. The observer’s shift changes the position and velocity of each individual object but not their simultaneous separation.

  1. Declare observer, object, axis and global time before assigning signs. Relative position and velocity are only well-defined after fixing the measurement frame and coordinate direction.
  2. Translate each position segment into a signed velocity (slope) and verify by graph area. Velocity is the rate of position change; integrating a constant signed velocity across a segment gives that segment's displacement.
  3. Derive the encounter time within the first valid interval and test it against the interval bounds. An equation inherits the domain of the motion conditions from which it was constructed; an out-of-domain root is inadmissible.
  4. Construct the second interval's position law continuously from its boundary and re-solve the encounter. The object's position does not jump merely because it changes speed or direction at the transition.

3. Worked examples

FOUNDATION · worked example

Problem: A bus moves east at 8 m/s. A passenger walks west at 2 m/s relative to the bus.

Reasoning: Passenger ground velocity is +8−2=+6 m/s.

Independent check: From the bus the passenger is westbound, from the ground eastbound.

CONNECTED · worked example

Problem: A cart is stationary at ground x=9 m. An observer starts at x=1 m and moves east at 2 m/s for 3 s.

Reasoning: Relative position changes from 8 to 2 m; relative velocity is −2 m/s and displacement −6 m.

Independent check: Ground displacement 0 minus observer displacement 6 gives −6 m.

ADVANCED · worked example

Problem: A moves east from x=0 at 4 m/s for 4 s then stops; B starts x=40 m and moves west at 3 m/s. When do they meet?

Reasoning: For 0≤t≤4, 4t=40−3t yields t=40/7≈5.71 s, outside its validity. After 4 s A is at x=16, so 16=40−3t gives t=8 s and position x=16 m.

Independent check: Both actual position models give x=16 at t=8; the first-stage root is rejected as out-of-domain.

4. Model limits and misconception repair

  • Elementary constant-velocity segment equations are not correct over an interval of continuously changing velocity.
  • A graph's signed area gives displacement, not necessarily total distance.
  • Relative velocity subtraction requires consistent frames and sign conventions.
  • A valid equation root still requires the event to fall within that equation's physical interval.
  • An object's displacement and distance generally depend on the observer's motion; simultaneous separation and a shared meeting event are invariant under a common translating frame.
Wrong model: Treat an algebraic meeting time as valid even when its first-stage motion assumption has ended.

Diagnostic: If equation xA=4t is valid only for t≤4 s, is the solution t=5.71 s a real first-stage meeting?

Repair: Reject the out-of-domain candidate, construct xA=16 for t≥4, then recompute and verify both positions.

5. Test your understanding

A cart moves +5 m/s; an observer moves +2 m/s along the same axis. If their separation is 12 m with the cart behind, when does the cart catch the observer?

Reveal textbook explanation

Relative closing speed 3 m/s; meeting in 4 s. Both coordinate systems describe the same simultaneous separation and meeting.

Existing Core2 question preparation

Moving observer and a reflected signal

Use ground x=0 at emission and a fixed reflector x=L. A pulse travels out at speed c and returns at the same speed while its receiver moves toward the reflector at constant speed U. The total path before return is L+(L−Ut)=2L−Ut, so ct=2L−Ut and t=2L/(c+U). Check that the return happens before the receiver reaches the reflector. Example: L=100 m, c=40 m/s and U=10 m/s give t=4 s, reflection at 2.5 s and receiver x=40 m. The pulse covers 100+60=160 m. Sound frequency and wavelength require the separate sound construction.

For a fixed reflector at L, returning signal x=2L−ct is valid only after t=L/c. Match it to the receiver position in each stage. Example L=50 m, c=20 m/s; receiver travels east 4 m/s for 2 s then rests at x=8. Constant-speed root 100/24 is after 2 s, invalid; stationary return t=(100−8)/20=4.6 s, after reflection at 2.5 s.
