## C13.01 — Oxidation and reduction in observed reactions

### The observation or contradiction

Black copper(II) oxide loses oxygen when hydrogen is passed over heated copper oxide, while water forms. Which substance has been reduced, and which one has been oxidised? The answer is not simply the name of the black solid.

### Build the explanatory model

At the school level, oxidation often means oxygen gain or hydrogen loss, while reduction means oxygen loss or hydrogen gain. In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, oxygen originally belonging to CuO ends up in water. CuO loses oxygen and is reduced; H₂ gains oxygen and is oxidised. These linked changes form one redox reaction. Every element remains accounted for, so a material ledger is more informative than colour alone.

The terms oxidising agent and reducing agent describe the effects on the *other* species. CuO is the oxidising agent here because it supplies oxygen to hydrogen and is itself reduced. Hydrogen is the reducing agent because it removes oxygen from copper oxide and is itself oxidised. A learner who assumes the reducing agent must itself be reduced has reversed this relationship.

Redox can also be expressed through electron transfer and oxidation numbers, but do not require those advanced ideas to explain an ordinary oxygen-transfer school experiment. Separate a balanced candidate equation from evidence of actual product identities. A black solid turning reddish suggests a transformation, but neither colour uniquely identifies CuO or copper without an independent test.

### Complete worked construction

Construct a particle diagram of one CuO unit interacting with one H₂ molecule. The product inventory is one Cu atom and one H₂O molecule. Cu 1, O 1 and H 2 are conserved. State explicitly: oxygen moves from CuO to H₂; oxygen gain for H₂ means oxidation, oxygen loss for CuO means reduction. Repeat the model with ZnO + C → Zn + CO as a contrast: ZnO is reduced and carbon is oxidised under that net equation.

### Misconception and repair

Wrong path: 'The reducing agent gains electrons in the same reaction because it is called reducing.' Repair by defining an agent according to what it does to the other species and checking the agent's own net chemical change.

### Boundary or independent check

A colour-changing indicator may not involve redox at all. Oxidation-state or species evidence must support the conclusion rather than relying on colour alone.

### Three distinct reconstruction demands

1. **EXPLAIN, candidate D3** — Explain oxygen transfer and the paired nature of reduction/oxidation in CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O.
2. **APPLY, candidate D3** — Apply the oxygen-transfer model to ZnO + C → Zn + CO, identifying both roles.
3. **JUSTIFY, candidate D3** — A dark solid turns reddish during heating. A student identifies both oxide reduction and the gas product from colour alone. Refute the reasoning and propose independent tests.

## C13.02 — Electron transfer and formal oxidation numbers

### The observation or contradiction

A charge-balanced ionic reaction can involve oxidation even when no oxygen atom is present. How can we follow the change while avoiding the mistake that an oxidation number is always a real ionic charge?

### Build the explanatory model

An ionic species Fe²⁺ can lose an electron to become Fe³⁺: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Losing electrons is oxidation. A separate reduction half-process must accept electrons in a full redox reaction. This electron ledger complements the oxygen-transfer language. For a genuine monatomic ion, the oxidation number equals its charge. For atoms in many covalent molecules, oxidation numbers are **formal bookkeeping values**, assigned by convention to track electron-accounting changes rather than directly measured local charges.

Oxidation-number conventions include: an uncombined element has formal oxidation number zero; a monatomic ion uses its ionic charge; oxygen is commonly −2 in ordinary oxides but has important exceptions; hydrogen is commonly +1 with nonmetals and −1 in metal hydrides. The sum of formal oxidation numbers equals the net species charge when counted with atom multiplicities. These rules need stated species and bonding context.

Moving from a reaction formula to an oxidation-number diagram is a REPRESENT or APPLY challenge. Write the formula, assign an oxidation number for each element under the appropriate conventions, check the sum, and then track changes across reactants/products. Do not infer that the assigned numbers give a microscopic mechanism; a one-electron numerical change does not prove a free electron travelled through a solution in one elementary step.

### Complete worked construction

Check iron(II) and iron(III): Fe²⁺ has formal oxidation number +2, Fe³⁺ +3; oxidation entails losing one electron. For water H₂O, using H +1 each and O −2 yields 2(+1)+(−2)=0, consistent with neutral water. For a covalent molecule this does not mean oxygen physically exists as a free O²⁻ ion inside each water molecule.

### Misconception and repair

Wrong path: 'All atoms with oxidation number +2 physically carry +2 ionic charge.' Repair by distinguishing formal assignment from actual ionic species and the molecular bonding model.

### Boundary or independent check

Oxygen in peroxides is often −1, so a blindly applied O = −2 rule can break the species-charge sum. Boundary conditions decide the model.

### Three distinct reconstruction demands

1. **EXPLAIN, candidate D3** — Explain why Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻ is oxidation without mentioning oxygen atoms.
2. **APPLY, candidate D3** — Apply oxidation-number rules to H₂O and H₂O₂, specifying why oxygen differs.
3. **JUSTIFY, candidate D3** — A student says carbon's +4 oxidation number in CO₂ proves it exists as isolated C⁴⁺ ions in the gas. Refute the claim.

## C13.03 — Balancing redox through half-reactions (EXTENSION) · EXTENSION (noncompulsory)

### The observation or contradiction

An ionic equation balances every chemical element but has different total charge on each side. Can such an equation represent a complete reaction? The missing condition is charge conservation.

### Build the explanatory model

The ion–electron method separates oxidation and reduction into half-reactions, balances all atoms and total charge, and then cancels the electrons transferred. In acidic medium, H₂O can balance oxygen and H⁺ can balance hydrogen; electrons balance charge. In basic medium, OH⁻ and water can be used consistently after a suitable transformation. The medium is not optional decoration, because the species available for balancing differ.

Consider Fe²⁺ oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. A permanganate reduction in acidic solution can be expressed as MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Each side has one Mn, four O and eight H. Charge on the left is −1 + 8 − 5 = +2, equal to Mn²⁺ on the right. Multiplying the iron half-reaction by five cancels electrons when the two are added.

The combined acidic-medium equation is MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺. Left charge: −1 + 8 + 10 = +17. Right charge: +2 + 15 = +17. Oxygen, hydrogen, iron and manganese counts also match. Such half-reaction bookkeeping is **EXTENSION** beyond the normal Class X redox introduction and must not be presented as compulsory NSEJS knowledge.

### Complete worked construction

Start with the combined equation and test two invariants independently: element ledger and charge ledger. If an author balances only oxygen and iron but forgets charge, the chemical model is incomplete. If a student changes Fe³⁺ into Fe²⁺ simply to force charge balance, they have changed the species rather than adjusted legitimate coefficients.

### Misconception and repair

Wrong path: 'A redox equation is balanced once each element count matches.' Repair by checking total charge in ionic equations, electron cancellation and the stated acidic/basic medium.

### Boundary or independent check

Using the acidic MnO₄⁻ to Mn²⁺ half-reaction unchanged in strongly basic conditions is invalid without medium-specific chemistry and appropriate products.

### Three distinct reconstruction demands

1. **EXPLAIN, candidate D3** — Explain why atoms and charge must both balance in the acidic permanganate/iron(II) reaction.
2. **APPLY, candidate D3** — Apply the half-reaction method to Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ and MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺ in acidic medium.
3. **JUSTIFY, candidate D3** — An equation balances Mn, Fe and O but charge differs. Refute a proposed balancing by changing the ionic species labels.

## C13.04 — Redox amounts and reaction-specific equivalence (EXTENSION) · EXTENSION (noncompulsory)

### The observation or contradiction

A student claims one mole of any oxidising reagent always accepts the same number of electrons. Why can even the same named reagent have different electron accounting in different media?

### Build the explanatory model

A balanced redox equation gives mole relationships between reactants; electron equivalents describe how many electrons a species gains or loses under a **specified net change**. Equivalent amount is thus reaction-specific rather than a constant label attached once to a bottle. For example, MnO₄⁻ reduced to Mn²⁺ involves a five-electron change per Mn under acidic conditions, but other permitted products can have a different electron change. The medium, products and source identities must be stated.

Use amount-of-substance reasoning with a balanced equation before introducing equivalent terminology. In the acidic permanganate–iron(II) equation, one mole of MnO₄⁻ reacts stoichiometrically with five moles of Fe²⁺. If the Fe²⁺ sample contains 0.050 mol, ideal complete conversion requires 0.010 mol permanganate under the stated chemistry. The calculation assumes Fe²⁺ is the only reducing agent and there are no interfering side reactions.

An exam-style table may show reactant concentration and consumption without revealing the medium. A defensible learner should refuse to assign an unqualified factor until the actual reduced species is known. Mathematical neatness is not enough: an incorrect chemistry model can produce a perfectly consistent but false answer.

### Complete worked construction

A 25.0 mL aliquot contains 0.0200 mol/L Fe²⁺, so amount Fe²⁺ is 0.0250 L × 0.0200 mol/L = 5.00×10⁻⁴ mol. Under the authenticated acidic Fe²⁺/MnO₄⁻ reaction, permanganate needed is one fifth: 1.00×10⁻⁴ mol. At 0.0100 mol/L MnO₄⁻, the theoretical volume is 0.0100 L = 10.0 mL. Verify that every unit and electron ratio is consistent. This is **EXTENSION**, not an ordinary Class X pH problem.

### Misconception and repair

Wrong path: 'Apply the familiar 5-equivalent factor to every permanganate reaction.' Repair by identifying the product oxidation state and medium, then balancing the specific reaction before computing.

### Boundary or independent check

If an oxidisable impurity is also present, the observed oxidant consumption cannot be assigned uniquely to Fe²⁺. Composition and blank controls must be specified.

### Three distinct reconstruction demands

1. **EXPLAIN, candidate D3** — Explain why permanganate's reaction-specific electron factor can depend on its reduction product.
2. **APPLY, candidate D3** — Apply the acidic reaction ratio to 0.050 mol Fe²⁺ and determine permanganate amount.
3. **JUSTIFY, candidate D3** — A titration record gives oxidant volume but omits pH and reaction products. Justify why a unique equivalent factor cannot be assumed.

## C13.05 — Formal volumetric analysis and measurement (ENRICHMENT) · ENRICHMENT (noncompulsory)

### The observation or contradiction

A burette shows a reproducible colour endpoint, and a student declares that the true chemical equivalence point must have been reached exactly. What is missing from that conclusion?

### Build the explanatory model

Volumetric analysis determines an amount through solution volume, concentration and a balanced reaction. A standard solution of known concentration is delivered from a burette into an accurately measured aliquot of sample. At the **equivalence point**, the amounts satisfy the stoichiometric relation; at a visible **indicator endpoint**, the colour criterion has changed. These are not always identical because indicators respond over a range and observations have experimental uncertainty.

Read a burette consistently at eye level and distinguish initial from final readings. Use an appropriate endpoint indicator, known aliquot and balanced species-specific reaction, ideally with a blank or repeated concordant trials. Errors can include overshoot, parallax, air bubbles in the tip, contaminated glassware and interfering reactions. A set of different trial readings must not be averaged blindly if a trial is clearly compromised.

An acid-base example is simpler than an advanced redox titration: for HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O under complete neutralisation, the acid/base mole ratio is 1:1. If 25.0 mL of 0.100 mol/L NaOH requires 20.0 mL HCl to reach a suitable equivalence approximation, then base amount is 0.00250 mol and acid concentration is 0.00250/0.0200 = 0.125 mol/L. The calculation still assumes a proper endpoint and no interfering acid/base reactions.

### Complete worked construction

Compare two trial burette differences of 20.0 and 23.8 mL. The discrepancy is too large to hide by quoting a neat mean without investigating technique or reagent chemistry. Recheck readings, tip bubbles, sample mixing, endpoint recognition and potential titrant instability. Formal titration is deliberately labelled **ENRICHMENT** in this NSEJS textbook design; learners should never conduct chemical titrations involving hazardous acids or oxidants unsupervised.

### Misconception and repair

Wrong path: 'Every indicator colour change establishes exact equivalence and a unique sample composition.' Repair by distinguishing indicator response from stoichiometric equality and asking whether side reactions or contamination occur.

### Boundary or independent check

Changing indicator can shift the apparent endpoint even if the same solution pair is used. The most appropriate indicator depends on the acid-base or redox system.

### Three distinct reconstruction demands

1. **EXPLAIN, candidate D3** — Explain the distinction between an indicator endpoint and a chemical equivalence point.
2. **APPLY, candidate D3** — Apply the 1:1 HCl/NaOH relation to 25.0 mL 0.100 mol/L NaOH requiring 20.0 mL HCl.
3. **JUSTIFY, candidate D3** — Two volumetric trials differ by almost 20%. Justify an investigation before accepting the mean as the final concentration.

## Integrated chapter conclusion

An experiment gives **observations**; a chemical equation is a **species model**; an electron/oxidation-number ledger is a **conservation accounting representation**. Do not turn one into the other without justification. The ordinary Class X lesson must remain intelligible without half-reaction balancing or titration, while those advanced methods remain explicitly labelled EXTENSION/ENRICHMENT. Source questions, official keys and experimental claims are independent custody requirements not fulfilled by this authored manuscript.
